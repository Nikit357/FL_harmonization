## 334 harmonization quality metrics analysis of 2,234 approaches in a multi-platform B-cell lymphoma bulk transcriptomic benchmark: ComBat is superior by cross-batch prediction quality

Daniil Nikitin^1,2^*, and Arsen Arakelyan^1^

^1^Institute of Molecular Biology, National Academy of Science of the Republic of Armenia, Yerevan, 0014, Armenia

^2^[affiliation 2: as in the published article]

*Corresponding author. Email: danya.nikitin.orel@gmail.com

**Keywords:** batch effect, transcriptomic harmonization, benchmarking metrics, cross-validation bias, leave-one-batch-out prediction, follicular lymphoma, gene expression

### Abstract

**Background.** Cross-platform transcriptomic harmonization benchmarks rely on individual success metric each: correlation with a reference profile, classifier accuracy, or a composite imported from single-cell integration. We found no published study that computes multiple orthogonal metric classes over one set of harmonization approaches and measures overlapping between the best approaches by these metrics.

**Methods.** We used the 2,234 approaches of the recent real-world benchmark: 31 harmonization methods, 14 batch-removal strategies, 3 imputations and 2 post-removal settings on 7,174 B-cell lymphoma transcriptomes. We assigned its 87 polarity-adjusted metrics to five classes (global, local, distributional, structural, and the composite group which is a mean of all the previous metrics) and added three biology-facing classes: within-cohort marker correlation (L), cross-batch rank agreement of a marker panel (M), and leave-one-batch-out (LOBO) prediction of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, follicular lymphoma and normal B cells (N). Each class resulted in top 112 approaches (5%) by its own rank-percentile composite, and we measured overlap by the Jaccard index.

**Results.** Harmonization methods of high harshness such as quantile normalization matched different batches to the same shape of distribution but were inferior by local and global metrics. Local and composite metrics resulted in the largest overlap of best approaches (0.333, 56 shared). Classes L and M, M and N - shared no approach, whereas L and N shared one [[COMMENT-2-START]]approach[[COMMENT-2-END]][[comment:2]]. The 15 approaches selected by the published clustermap shared none of the 112 that class N elected. Class N resulted in the ComBat family in 84 of its 112 places and class M elected [[COMMENT-3-START]]two methods [[COMMENT-3-END]][[comment:3]]whose median marker correlation was -0.0007 and -0.0082. [[COMMENT-4-START]]Of the 32,746 three-class LOBO folds, 15,436 (47.1%) carried one class and reached a median macro F1 of 0.903, against 0.7334 on the rest.[[COMMENT-4-END]][[comment:4]]

**Conclusions.** Best transcriptomic harmonization approach is highly contingent on the quality metrics used. We provide a six-step recipe for metric classes, their thresholds and the cross-batch tests for the robust selection of best approaches.

### Introduction

Technical variation between sequencing platforms, RNA extraction protocols and tissue preservation methods separates samples of the same biological class, and it distorts inference whenever it is correlated with the outcome under study (Leek et al. 2010) . Algorithms that remove this variation from bulk expression matrices have been initially developed microarray integration (Lazar et al. 2013), whereas the cross-platform harmonization is more challenging, particularly, between microarrays and next-generation sequencing (NGS) (Borisov and Buzdin 2022) .

Each benchmark of these algorithms declares a different criterion of success. An early 2021 empirical comparison of cross-platform normalization scored methods on inter-platform concordance of gene lists supported by ROC-like curves and bootstrap (Rudy and Valafar 2011). A later comparison scored the methods by the accuracy of a downstream subtype classifier (Thompson et al. 2016). The single-cell integration literature supplied a third criterion, a trade-off between batch effect removal and biology conservation (Luecken et al. 2021). A benchmark of batch-effect correction methods for single-cell RNA sequencing recorded the same trade-off, reporting also that quality metrics can disagree with visual inspection of the embeddings (Tran et al. 2020). Two of the metrics that this literature contributed, the k-nearest-neighbour batch-effect test (Büttner et al. 2018)  and the local inverse Simpson’s index (Korsunsky et al. 2019), are now applied to bulk data, albeit there is still no standard of harmonization quality measurement (Borisov and Buzdin 2022).

The harmonization quality criteria are especially important in datasets where the biological contrast is small compared to the batch level technical differences. In the case of germinal center (GC) B-cell lymphomas, expression profiling splits diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) into germinal-centre and activated B-cell types (Alizadeh et al. 2000) predicting survival after chemotherapy (Rosenwald et al. 2002). Survival in follicular lymphoma (FL) is predicted by subtle differences in immune-cell signatures of the microenvironment (Dave et al. 2004), and a 23-gene score separates FL patients into two outcome groups under immunochemotherapy (Huet et al. 2018). These small-scale transcriptomic differences are challenging to survive transfer between materials and platforms, explaining the[[COMMENT-6-START]] cell-of-origin assignment reinvention for formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded[[COMMENT-6-END]][[comment:6]] (FFPE) tissue (Scott et al. 2014) and multiple cohort sources for genetic subtypes of DLBCL (Chapuy et al. 2018; Schmitz et al. 2018). The same transfer problem governs expression signatures outside lymphoma, for example microsatellite instability status (Sorokin et al. 2021), therapy prescription in colorectal (Sorokin et al. 2022) and kidney cancer (Gudkov et al. 2022) and DNA repair pathway activation in thyroid tumors (Vladimirova et al. 2021). In a recent T-cell lymphoma xenograft study, principal-component analysis (PCA) showed only a partial overlap between primary tumors and their matched xenografts, which the authors attributed to tumor content and host cells, and that variation was removed by surrogate variable analysis (SVA) (Fiore et al. 2025).

In case of the real-world batch correction scenario, the analysis is complicated by confounding between the batch variable and with the class labels (Soneson et al. 2014) which drastically reduces predictors generalizability (Parker and Leek 2012). Moreover, cross-validation could be biased under these scenarios (Varma and Simon 2006) which is amplified by selection bias among thousands of genes in transcriptomic datasets containing only tens to hundreds of samples (Ambroise and McLachlan 2002). These issues lead to prediction failures of models deployed in clinical practice (Saeb et al. 2017; Whalen et al. 2021), as highlighted as ‘reproducibility crisis’ in the machine learning science in general (Kapoor and Narayanan 2023) .

To address these current issues in transcriptomic cross-platform harmonization, we deeply analyzed the 2,234 harmonization approaches of a recently published multi-platform B-cell lymphoma benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17), which calculate 344 quality metrics across 31 methods, 14 prior batch-removal strategies, 3 imputation schemes and 2 post-removal settings. For each metric class among local, global, distributional, composite, biomarker genes correlation before and after harmonization (class L), cross-batch agreement of biomarker genes (class M) and leave-one-batch-out (LOBO) prediction metrics (class N), we extensively compared harmonization methods and batch removal strategies, selecting 5% best approaches by a given metric class. Finally, we propose a novel six step framework for harmonization quality metrics usage to select best harmonization approaches in other bulk trascriptomic datasets, including the non-B cell lymphoma ones.

### Materials and methods

#### Dataset, harmonization approaches and the analysis set

The GC B-cell lymphomas dataset, the 14 prior batch-removal strategies, the 3 imputation schemes, the harmonization method registry and the optional post-removal step are specified in the benchmarking study previously published by our team (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17): 7,174 bulk transcriptomes from 88 germinal-centre B-cell lymphoma cohorts across RNA sequencing and Affymetrix, Illumina and Agilent microarrays. We re-used that output and computed no new harmonization. The most important algorithms were ComBat with its reference (Combat-ref) and count-based (Combat-seq) variants (Johnson et al. 2007; Zhang et al. 2020), surrogate variable analysis (Leek and Storey 2007), mutual nearest neighbors (Haghverdi et al. 2018), feature-specific quantile normalization (Franks et al. 2018), cross-platform normalization by XPN (Shabalin et al. 2008)  and Shambhala-2 (Borisov et al. 2019; Borisov et al. 2022); the full registry with the parameters used is given in the source benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17).

A harmonization approach is defined as one combination of batch-removal strategy, imputation, harmonization method and post-removal. We defined the approaches set by the condition that fewer than 5% of samples carry no defined expression value for all genes (pct_samples_allNA < 5). This set contained 2,234 approaches over 31 methods, 14 strategies, 3 imputations and both post-removal settings. Two methods, 34_arsyn and 38_harman, had a minimum of 74.23% of samples with no defined expression across all 84 of their approaches and were not included into the final approach set. Shambhala-2 was implemented using its standard P0 and Q0 calibration datasets and was named as 20_shambhala in the benchmark.

#### Metric classes and the best approaches selection procedure

We assigned each of the 87 polarity-adjusted scoring metrics of the source benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17)  to one of four classes: global, covering principal component regression (PCReg), the dispersion separability criterion (DSC), per-component R^2^ and the variance explained by each principal component; local, covering the k-nearest-neighbour batch-effect test (kBET), the integration and cell-type local inverse Simpson’s indices (iLISI and cLISI), average silhouette width and graph connectivity; distributional, covering the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistics and per-gene batch quantities; and structural, covering tSNE and UMAP embedding statistics, intra- and inter-group distances, the distance ratio and the Watermelon score [[COMMENT-14-START]](Supplementary File X)[[COMMENT-14-END]][[comment:14]]. A fifth class, the composite, is the equally weighted mean of those four classes.

Within a class we converted every metric to a rank percentile over the 2,234 approaches, oriented so that a higher percentile is better according to the polarity assignments from the benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17), and averaged the percentiles into one class score per approach ([[COMMENT-15-START]]Supplementary File X[[COMMENT-15-END]][[comment:15]]). For each class we took top 5% approaches as the best ones (112 approaches in total). For comparison, we treated the 15 approaches selected by the published clustermap (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17). Overlap between each two elected sets was calculated as Jaccard index.

We also built a separate generalizability and biology preservation index as the mean of six rank percentiles: [[COMMENT-16-START]]the full and multiclass-only LOBO three-class macro F1, the two-class LOBO macro area under the curve (AUC), [[COMMENT-16-END]][[comment:16]]the mean within-cohort marker correlation before and after harmonization, the group M cross-batch agreement of biomarker genes subtracted by the unharmonized baseline, and the [[COMMENT-17-START]]worst multiclass fold[[COMMENT-17-END]][[comment:17]]. We compared batch-removal strategies against each other, imputations against each other and harmonization methods against each other, and the clustermap-selected group from the benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17) was treated as an isolated named group.

#### The biology evaluating metric classes L, M and N

Since gene signatures were systematically shown to predict biological subtypes better than individual genes (Jovčevska et al. 2019; Sorokin et al. 2020) , for classes L and M, we took 633-gene marker panel assembled from published FL and DLBCL studies and its narrow 56-gene versions as described in the benchmarking study (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17). In the L class, the Spearman correlation of these gene’s expression before and after harmonization, together with the contrast against a housekeeping control (Risso et al. 2014), were calculated. We treated a cohort wise mean marker correlation before and after harmonization above 0.75 as a necessary, albeit not sufficient, condition for good quality harmonization approach.

For class M metric, we calculated Spearman rank correlation of the marker panel between pairs of batches, computed separately for pairs of the same biological group and for pairs of different groups (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17). The margin was calculated as the same-biology agreement subtracted by the different-biology agreement. We also analyzed the margin relative to the unharmonized baseline expression matrices at the same strategy and imputation, so we were able to extract approaches that increased same-biology agreement and decreased different-biology agreement against their own baseline.

Class N metrics were designed as leave-one-batch-out prediction to measure a transferability and generatability of the best harmonization approaches selected. For each approach and each of the 24 RNA batches we excluded this batch and fitted logistic regression in principal components on the training batches alone for the three-class target (DLBCL, FL and normal B cells) and for the two-class target (FL against DLBCL). Then we projected the PCA coordinates at the held-out batch and predicted its biology labels using the previously fitted logistic regression, calculating Area Under Curve (AUC) and F1 scores. We also controlled this calculation using 100 biological label permutations and averaged all the N class metrics separately on multiclass batches (having more than one biology class) and on all batches. Multiclass-only batches were selected in order to remove a potential F1/AUC bias in batches that contain single biology group.

#### Statistics and software

Group comparisons across three or more groups used the [[COMMENT-20-START]]Kruskal-Wallis H test[[COMMENT-20-END]][[comment:20]], and monotone associations used the Spearman correlation coefficient. Per-strategy coefficients were analyzed [[COMMENT-21-START]]as their ranges and medians[[COMMENT-21-END]][[comment:21]] and selected groups were reported by their medians and median absolute deviations (MAD). We ran the analyses under Python 3.11 with pandas 2.3.3, numpy 1.26.4, scipy 1.12.0, scikit-learn 1.3.2, statsmodels 0.14.1, matplotlib 3.10.8 and seaborn 0.13.2.

#### AI usage

Claude Code with the Claude Opus 5 model was used for literature retrieval, analysis code and language checks, in a plan-then-act mode: the agent wrote an implementation plan, the authors reviewed and corrected it over several cycles, and only then were the code and documents written. The study design, the choice of comparisons and the interpretation are the authors’ own.

### Results and discussion

#### Metric classes capture non-overlapping components of harmonization quality

We started the analysis from the two metric families that dominate the published comparisons, the global PCA-based and the local neighborhood and investigated their order in the canonical set of 2,234 approaches. As the global representative we took PCReg in polarity-adjusted form: 1 means that the annotation column explains none of the PCA variation and 0 means that it explains all of it. Plotting PCReg for the biology column against PCReg for the batch column gave a weak negative association across the whole set (Spearman r = -0.159, p = 3.7 x 10^-14^, Figure 1A and 1B for harmonization methods and batch removal strategies, respectively). The 15 clustermap-selected approaches occupied the upper right of the plot, with a batch median of 0.871 (MAD 0.122) against 0.626 for the remaining approaches and a biology median of [[COMMENT-25-START]]0.820 (MAD 0.063) against 0.802[[COMMENT-25-END]][[comment:25]].

The initial screening of harmonization approaches by global, local and distributional metrics showed that the three families ordered the same set of approaches differently. In the next chapters we deeply investigated these differences in order to derive a unified framework of harmonization approaches benchmarking.

#### Global variance-based metrics saturate below the threshold of biological preservation

Twelve of the 31 methods overlapped batches better than biology in PCA space, and five of them (03_limma, 07_pycombat, 13_fsmvn, 28_npn and 33_amdbnorm) reached a mean batch PCReg at least 0.999, with 33_amdbnorm at exactly 1.0000 (Supplementary Figure 1A). At strategy level only two of the 14 strategies, RNA-seq-only and Affymetrix-only, overlapped batches better than biology (Supplementary Figure 1B), which places the choice of method above the choice of strategy at this level of batch effect quality control.

Looking at the PCReg-saturated approaches, we derived that of the 198 approaches whose batch PCReg exceeded 0.9999, generated by six methods (03_limma, 13_fsmvn, 07_pycombat, 21_harmonizr, 33_amdbnorm and 28_npn[[COMMENT-28-START]]), 98.5% returned a kBET acceptance rate under 0.1[[COMMENT-28-END]][[comment:28]] ([[COMMENT-29-START]]Supplementary Figure 6E[[COMMENT-29-END]][[comment:29]]) indicating poor local batch separation.

The remaining global metrics closely reproduced PCReg and added little separation. DSC for the batch column followed PCReg along a monotone sigmoid (Supplementary Figure 3C), and DSC for the platform column followed DSC for the batch column linearly on a logarithmic scale ([[COMMENT-30-START]]Supplementary Figure 3D[[COMMENT-30-END]][[comment:30]]). All 15 clustermap approaches were below the overall median batch DSC of 0.962, although the four MNN approaches (0.781 to 0.957) located at the boundary, about two orders of magnitude higher than the FSQN R approaches (0.0013 to 0.0098). Such behavior can be explained by the definition of DSC as between-group scatter divided by within-group scatter, which is sensitive to outliers. Average silhouette width tracked PCReg [[COMMENT-31-START]]in a noisy manner[[COMMENT-31-END]][[comment:31]], with the Affymetrix-only and FFPE-only strategies worst (means of 0.165 and 0.078, against a range of -0.066 to -0.019 elsewhere, Supplementary Figure 3I) and 02_median_scaling and 33_amdbnorm best (-0.286 and -0.276, Supplementary Figure 3J).

The two pipeline factors, namely imputation and post-removal, impacted the harmonization performance to the least extent according to the source benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17), and we quantified them here on the global metric. Softimpute exceeded k-nearest-neighbor (KNN) imputation by between 1.7 and 9.7 percentage points, with a mean of 4.8, across the 11 methods with the lowest batch PCReg, while strict exclusion and [[COMMENT-32-START]]k-nearest-neighbour [[COMMENT-32-END]][[comment:32]]imputation differed by 0.020 in mean batch PCReg over the affected methods (0.370 against 0.390, Supplementary Figure 2A). Post-removal raised the mean batch PCReg for 30 of the 31 methods, with a median shift of 0.030; 22_tmm increased to the highest degree, from 0.70 up to 0.88, and 23_vst from 0.70 to 0.83. 12_scanorama was the single method with PCReg decrease, from 0.49 to 0.48 (Supplementary Figure 2C).

Batch removal strategies impacted batch PCReg to higher degree compared to imputations and post removals: every method reached at least 0.76 under the RNA-seq-only strategy, 0.72 under Affymetrix-only and 0.46 under Affymetrix-extended (Supplementary Figure 2B). In contrast, 16_fsqn_r method decreased from a cross-strategy (S0_no_removal) mean of 0.83 to 0.30 under microarray-only and 0.08 under FFPE-only, proving that feature-specific quantile normalization perform poorly in cross-platform FFPE-only dataset.

For all the batch removal strategies we also investigated batch-explained variance for the first 10 PCs (Supplementary Figure 2F), examining the percentage of variance explained and the R2 for RNA_BATCH at each PC. The joint group of the clustermap best approaches was the second one after the C strategy (shown earlier in Supplementary Figure 1B). At the heatmap both the strategy C and the clustermap best approaches group showed the lowest average percentage of variance explained by the first PC (30.4 and 37.9%, respectively), and the smoothest decay of the percentage by PC’s number. The G strategy showed the highest average percentage, 66.0%, presumably because the already quantile-normalized SOM cohort makes up a larger share of the samples in this strategy than in any other. Taking together, global metrics in-depth analysis showed that global metrics form a weak, permissive criterion of harmonization quality. Both biology and batch PCReg should be measured, and other metric classes should be added to evaluate harmonization quality robustly.

#### Batch-associated variance is redistributed across principal components instead of being eliminated

We separately investigated distribution of batch-associated variance over principal components by batch removal strategies. Ten of the 14 strategies, and 19 of the 31 methods, placed a lower batch-explained R^2^ on the first principal component than on some later one [[COMMENT-35-START]](Figure 1C, Supplementary Figure 2F). [[COMMENT-35-END]][[comment:35]]Among the five methods whose explained variance decayed most slowly, four (excluding 20_shambhala) reached their maximum batch R^2^ away from the first component (Figure 1C). The clustermap group averaged 37.9% and 11.6% of total variance and 22.9% and 15.2% batch R^2^ on the first two components; 14_qsmooth, the weakest method on the PCA metrics, averaged 53.7% and 13.5% of variance with 90.8% and 67.1% batch R^2^; the unharmonized data averaged 73.5% and 9.7% of variance with 64.8% and 65.9% batch R^2^. The RNA-seq-only strategy and the clustermap group showed the lowest first-component variance (30.4% and 37.9%) among the batch removal strategies and the smoothest decay (Supplementary Figure 2F), whereas the Affymetrix-only strategy had the highest variance explained by the first component (66.0%). The individual principal components analysis therefore showed that harmonization benchmarking based on the first component only inherently underestimates batch effect, and harmonization methods can hide the batch-related variance into the lower rank principal components which should be accounted for during benchmarking.

#### Local neighborhood metrics discriminate well between good and bad approaches but are vulnerable to batch composition

Only six of the 31 methods (02_median_scaling, 10_mnn, 16_fsqn_r, 25_angel, 28_npn and 33_amdbnorm) mixed batches better than biology by LISI (Supplementary Figure 4A). The kBET acceptance rate behaved as more stringent criterion: it stayed under 0.01 for 82.0% of the 2,234 approaches and under 0.10 for 97.0% (Supplementary Figure 5A). Five of the 15 clustermap best approaches exceeded 0.10, and these approaches were SVA under the RNA-seq-only and FFPE-only strategies. Eight methods exceeded the unharmonized baseline of 0.027 by batch kBET acceptance rate: 22_tmm at 0.331 (RNA-seq only), 23_vst at 0.230 (RNA-seq only), 10_mnn at 0.044, 04_sva at 0.036, 36_explobatch at 0.036, 13_fsmvn at 0.035, 20_shambhala at 0.029 and 19_tdm at 0.028. The other 22 methods scored below raw dataset, indicating that they do not improve local batch mixing (Supplementary Figure 5C).

The malignant-only and FFPE-only strategies retained four malignant groups and returned the lowest biology LISI (medians 1.10 and 1.07), whereas the FF-only strategy preserved all the normal B-cell types and returned the highest value (median 1.62, Supplementary Figure 4B). The RNA-seq-only strategy produced a tight group of 41 approaches spanning 18 methods, with kBET between 0.654 and 0.867, and none of them was selected by the clustermap, because their LISI values stayed between 1.24 and 1.38 [[COMMENT-38-START]](Supplementary Figure 5B).[[COMMENT-38-END]][[comment:38]]

Taking together, the in-depth analysis of local metrics showed that reaching the maximum of one local metric is not sufficient for the clustermap best selection. Moreover, the local metrics were highly dependent on prior batch removal strategies. These findings indicate that a robust harmonization quality benchmarking pipeline should treat local metrics as necessary but not sufficient metric family.

#### Distributional convergence is the only axis on which the high harshness harmonization methods rank first

We further deeply compared metrics of distributional similarity between harmonization approaches. On the fraction of cohort pairs whose expression distributions differ by the KS test, 29 of the 31 methods had the score below the unharmonized baseline of 0.948, and only 27_dwd was worse than the raw expression mnatrix (0.965, Supplementary Figure 7A). Only ten methods brought the metric below 0.7, ranging from 0.173 for 28_npn and 0.217 for 26_xpn up to 0.691 for 03_limma, and the clustermap best group reached 0.560, so 56.0% of cohort pairs still differed after the best harmonization by the clustermap assessment. Distance ratio separated the clustermap group as well (batch distance ratio between 0.845 and 1.032, with a median of 0.914 against 0.757 over the full set, Supplementary Figure 7E), with the only the two methods, 27_dwd (0.508) and 14_qsmooth (0.531), having the metric below the unharmonized dataset (0.566). The Watermelon score [[COMMENT-40-START]]did not separate the clustermap group from the rest approaches[[COMMENT-40-END]][[comment:40]] [[COMMENT-41-START]](Supplementary Figure 7G to 7J), [[COMMENT-41-END]][[comment:41]]which makes this metric a candidate for an independent metric class to be compared to the global and local families.

We then compared methods by their harshness score defined in the harmonization benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17) as low harshness is linear transformation, high harshness is significant distribution reshaping (for example in quantile normalization) and medium harshness is everything in between. There were 344 low, 1,042 medium and 848 high harshness approaches for the comparison. Low-harshness methods demonstrated the highest batch PCReg under 11 strategies of 14, and the highest batch LISI under 10 of 14, while high-harshness methods showed lowest tSNE entropy under 12 strategies of 14 (Supplementary Figure 8A, 8C, 8D). High harshness prevailed over low and middle harshness on one axis only, namely distributional convergence, where it demonstrated the lowest KS fraction significant under 13 strategies of 14 (Supplementary Figure 8F). Matching expression distributions to a same distribution is a distinguishing feature of harshest transformations, but it does not lead to improvement of local or global metrics.

We also calculated and compared a composite score, which was the equally weighted composite of all 87 metrics. Expectedly, it ranked the clustermap group as first at 0.602, with the RNA-seq-only strategy next at the level of 0.578 (Supplementary Figure 9A). The composite score was dominated by global distance metrics, that supply 47 of the 87 columns and 0.360 of the group’s score while [[COMMENT-42-START]]varying by only 0.017 across strategies and 0.030 across methods[[COMMENT-42-END]][[comment:42]] (Supplementary Figure 9A, B, respectively). In contrast, local neighborhood metrics provide only 22 columns and 0.109 of the score but produce the largest between-group spreads, 0.043 across strategies and 0.049 across methods for the neighbor-based integration group (Supplementary Figure 9C, 9D, respectively). Batch-annotated columns supply 57 metrics of the 87 and between 60.7 and 67.4% of the composite score, and the 18 biology columns separate approaches [[COMMENT-43-START]]weakly[[COMMENT-43-END]][[comment:43]]: across strategies their spread is 0.053, and for the batch columns it is 0.122 (Supplementary Figure 9E, 9F, respectively).

We also sorted all approaches by the composite score and revealed an upper tail of 150 approaches above 0.6 (6.7% of total appricase), containing 9 of the 15 clustermap selections (Supplementary Figure 10). We did not reveal ordering of the harshness tiers by this composite score.

#### Preservation of biomarker expression and of cross-batch rank structure selects a distinct set of approaches

Since none of the 87 scoring metrics in the published benchmark assess expression of biomarker genes, we deeply studied performance of L and M metric classes. These classes were visualized for the clustermap best approach in the benchmarking article but there were not extensively compared between batch removal strategies and harmonization methods. Plotting raw against harmonized expression, gene by gene and cohort by cohort, shows what each class is reacting to: the unharmonized baseline lies on the identity line by construction ([[COMMENT-50-START]]Figure 4A[[COMMENT-50-END]][[comment:50]]), 13_fsmvn moves every point along a per-gene affine path and keeps the within-cohort order (Figure 4B), 12_scanorama returns a cloud with no relation to the input (Supplementary Figure 14A), and 29_combat_ref shifts the cohorts onto a common scale while keeping the within-cohort spread (Supplementary Figure 14B).

Class L is saturated at the top of its range. Five methods returned a median within-cohort marker correlation of exactly 1.0000 (01_raw, 36_explobatch, 31_ruv3prps, 27_dwd, 21_harmonizr), and the low-harshness tier sat at 0.998. When class L elects on its own it therefore chooses unharmonized output. Of its 112 elected approaches, 55 are 01_raw and 38 are 36_explobatch, so 93 of 112 places go to output that is unchanged or close to unchanged, with 13_fsmvn and 02_median_scaling taking 7 each, 04_sva 4 and 03_limma 1 (Supplementary File 1, sheet Method_census_by_class). Class M has the opposite failure. Its own composite is dominated by same-biology agreement, which rises whenever an algorithm makes all profiles alike, so class M elected only two methods: 10_mnn took 61 of its 112 places and 12_scanorama took 51. #### Cross-batch prediction elects a third set, whose ranking is stable across two cuts of the fold population

Class N asks whether a classifier fitted on the batches that are present can label the samples of a batch it has never seen. Across the analysis set this produced 32,746 three-class folds over 24 RNA batches. Of those, 15,436 (47.1%) contained a single biological class and reached a median macro F1 of 0.903, against 0.7334 over the 17,310 folds carrying two or more classes; inside the latter, two-class folds were the harder ones (median 0.629) and three-class folds the easier (median 0.804) (Figure 5C).

The fold population has to be restricted to the approaches under analysis before it is counted. The per-fold table holds 53,737 three-class folds over 3,738 run identifiers under 50 method names, and a census over all of them describes approaches this article never analyses: 20,991 of those folds fall outside the analysis set, of which 20,343 belong to the 17 non-canonical Shambhala parameter variants and the remaining 648 to 34_arsyn and 38_harman, the two methods whose approaches all fail the 5% all-missing criterion. The canonical Shambhala variant is retained and contributes 1,200 folds. An earlier count over the unrestricted table gave 53,737 folds with 25,426 single-class (47.3%) and fold-level medians of 0.952, 0.648 and 0.830 by class count. The counts quoted here come from the restricted population, and the conclusion is the same under both: about half of the held-out batches carry one class, and they score higher than the folds that require the classifier to separate classes.

The two cuts agree closely and do not coincide. Spearman correlation between the full-cut mean and the multiclass-only mean is 0.810 over the 2,174 approaches carrying both, and 0.815 over the 2,058 with at least three multiclass folds; an earlier value of 0.812 came from the unrestricted fold table. That is high enough for the two cuts to select the same methods and low enough for the order inside the leading group to change, which is why both are reported everywhere below.

Two fold structures make a high value uninformative, and we report the approaches that carry them with the structure stated beside the number. Fifty-nine of the 2,234 approaches have no multiclass fold at all, and 20 Affymetrix-only approaches with post-removal report a full-cut F1 of 1.000 with a perfect worst fold and zero multiclass folds (Figure 5C). Separately, the strategy that retains the known-bad batches gives the highest prediction values anywhere: A_confirmed_bad with strict exclusion under 29_combat_ref and post-removal reaches a full-cut F1 of 0.958, a multiclass-only mean of 0.924, a worst multiclass fold of 0.643 over 11 multiclass folds, a two-class AUC of 0.998 and a marker correlation of 0.957. If a retained bad batch is also diagnosis-pure, held-out prediction measures that confounding, so this approach is reported and is not offered as a recommendation. Protecting the biological covariate during correction does not remove the problem: when groups are unevenly distributed across batches, the corrected data are not batch-effect free and the estimation errors are deflated [52].

#### The sets elected by the individual metric classes show limited intersection

Running the election once per class over the same 2,234 candidates gives eight sets of 112 approaches and one published set of 15. The largest agreement between any two of them is between the local class and the equally weighted composite, Jaccard 0.333, which is 56 shared approaches (Figure 5E, Supplementary File 2, sheet Cross_election_matrix). The composite’s other overlaps follow from its construction as the mean of four classes: 0.325 with the distributional class (55 approaches), 0.295 with the structural class (51) and 0.217 with the global class (40). Between the four component classes themselves the agreement is lower: local and distributional share 51 approaches (Jaccard 0.295), global and distributional 27 (0.137), local and structural 17 (0.082), global and local 15 (0.072), global and structural 15 (0.072), and distributional and structural 12 (0.057). No pair of metric classes agrees on even a third of its choices.

The three biology-facing classes sit almost entirely outside that structure. Classes L and M share no approach, classes M and N share no approach, and classes L and N share one (Jaccard 0.0045). Class N shares nothing with the global class and nothing with the composite, and its largest overlap with any other class is 10 approaches with the structural class (0.047). Class M reaches 12 approaches with the local class (0.057) and 9 with the structural class (0.042). Class L reaches 9 approaches with the composite (0.042) and 7 with the distributional class (0.032).

The 15 approaches that the published clustermap selected share 9 approaches with the local class (Jaccard 0.076), 6 with the distributional class (0.050), 4 each with the global class and the composite (0.033), and 3 each with the structural class and class M (0.024). They share none with class L and none with class N. The set that a joint reading of 87 metrics produced therefore has no member in common with the set that cross-batch prediction elects, and none with the set that marker preservation elects.

The method censuses say why (Figure 5F, Figure 5G). The global class puts 86 of its 112 places into two methods, 13_fsmvn (59) and 03_limma (27), both of which drive batch PCReg to its ceiling. Class L puts 93 places into 01_raw and 36_explobatch. Class M puts all 112 into 10_mnn and 12_scanorama. Class N puts 84 into four ComBat implementations. The local, distributional, structural and composite classes spread over 24, 22, 20 and 24 methods respectively, and share their leading method, 16_fsqn_r, which takes 9, 12, 18 and 18 places. Four of the nine groups are therefore single-method verdicts, and they name four different methods.

This is the cost of choosing a criterion before running a benchmark. A study that reports global metrics would recommend 13_fsmvn, whose median full-cut LOBO F1 is 0.537 and whose median multiclass-only F1 is 0.624, both in the bottom five methods. A study that reports cross-batch rank agreement without the margin would recommend 10_mnn, whose median within-cohort marker correlation is -0.0007. A study that reports LOBO prediction alone would recommend ComBat-Seq implementations that return mean maximum expression values of 2.76 x 10^8^ and 1.46 x 10^11^. Each recommendation is correct under its own criterion and fails under the next one. The choice of metric class therefore determines the recommendation more than the data do.

The practical consequence is that a benchmark has to state its metric classes, its gates and its held-out test before it ranks anything, and has to report all of them afterwards. Class L is a gate that an approach must pass and cannot win on, because a per-batch monotone transform scores near 1.0 by construction and the low-harshness tier sits at 0.998 while predicting worst of the three tiers (0.631 on the full cut). Class M has to be read as a margin against the unharmonized baseline at the same strategy and imputation, because agreement alone rewards collapse. Class N has to be cut by batch and reported on both fold cuts, because 47.1% of the held-out batches carry one class and score a median of 0.903 against 0.7334 for the rest, and because a confounded strategy reaches the highest value of all (0.958 full cut, 0.924 multiclass-only). We have drawn these three traps and the six steps around them as a recipe (Figure 6), in the same terms the analysis above used.

### Conclusions

We ran the same election eight times over one set of 2,234 harmonization approaches, changing only the metric class that was allowed to vote, and the winners changed with it. The largest agreement between any two elected sets of 112 approaches was 56 approaches (Jaccard 0.333), and that pair was a class and the composite built from it. Between the three biology-facing classes the agreement was one approach in total: marker preservation and cross-batch rank agreement shared none, cross-batch rank agreement and prediction shared none, and marker preservation and prediction shared one. The 15 approaches selected by a joint reading of 87 metrics shared none of the 112 that prediction elected.

Each class elected what it was built to reward. Marker preservation elected unharmonized and near-unharmonized output, because a per-batch monotone transform leaves within-cohort ranks unchanged and scores near 1.0 by construction. Cross-batch rank agreement elected the two methods that make all profiles alike, whose median within-cohort marker correlation is -0.0007 and -0.0082. Global variance metrics elected the two methods that drive batch PCReg to its ceiling, one of which ranks in the bottom five on prediction. Cross-batch prediction elected four implementations of ComBat, two of which return mean maximum expression values of 2.76 x 10^8^ and 1.46 x 10^11^. Every one of these verdicts is defensible inside its own class and indefensible outside it.

Three properties of the metrics themselves caused most of that behaviour, and all three can be handled by reporting. A within-cohort correlation metric saturates, so it works as a gate at a stated threshold and does not order the approaches that pass: the low-harshness tier holds it at 0.998 while returning the lowest full-cut prediction F1 of the three tiers (0.631). An agreement metric rises whenever an algorithm converges profiles, so it has to be read as a margin between same-biology and different-biology pairs, taken against the unharmonized baseline at the same strategy and imputation; requiring both directions of that margin leaves 61 of 2,150 non-raw approaches (2.8%). A prediction metric depends on the composition of the held-out batch, so it has to be cut by batch and reported on both fold cuts: 15,436 of the 32,746 three-class folds carry one class and reach a median macro F1 of 0.903 against 0.7334 for the rest, and the highest prediction values anywhere come from a strategy that keeps the known-bad batches (full-cut F1 0.958, multiclass-only 0.924).

We set these out as a recipe (Figure 6). Declare the metric classes before computing them. Compute every class over the full approach set instead of a shortlist. Use the within-cohort correlation as a gate at a stated threshold. Rank on a margin taken against an explicit unharmonized baseline. Validate with leave-one-batch-out prediction whose projection is fitted on training batches only, reported on both fold cuts with the multiclass fold count beside each value. Report the elected set of every class together with their intersections, so a reader can see which recommendation depends on which criterion. Applied to this dataset the recipe leaves a small set of approaches that pass every step, led by no removal with softimpute under 29_combat_ref, which reaches a full-cut F1 of 0.946, a multiclass-only mean of 0.887 over 11 multiclass folds and a marker correlation of 0.880.

The limits of this work follow from its design. All 2,234 approaches come from one dataset of germinal-centre B-cell lymphomas, so the numbers are specific to a setting in which the biological contrast is small and the technical contrast is large. The election threshold of 5% is a choice, and a different threshold changes the set sizes although not the ordering of the overlaps. Classes L and M rest on one 633-gene panel, and a different panel would move their members. The prediction class uses three diagnostic classes and 24 batches, so its folds are coarse. What does not depend on these choices is the finding that the classes disagree, because that disagreement is measured between sets drawn from the same 2,234 candidates by the same procedure.
