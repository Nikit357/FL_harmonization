## 344 harmonization quality metrics analysis of 2,234 approaches in a multi-platform B-cell lymphoma bulk transcriptomic benchmark: ComBat is superior by cross-batch prediction quality

Daniil Nikitin1,2*, and Arsen Arakelyan1

1Institute of Molecular Biology, National Academy of Science of the Republic of Armenia, Yerevan, 0014, Armenia

2[affiliation 2: as in the published article]

*Corresponding author. Email: danya.nikitin.orel@gmail.com

Keywords: batch effect, transcriptomic harmonization, benchmarking metrics, cross-validation bias, leave-one-batch-out prediction, follicular lymphoma, gene expression

### Abstract

Background. Cross-platform transcriptomic harmonization benchmarks rely on individual success metric each: correlation with a reference profile, classifier accuracy, or a composite imported from single-cell integration. We found no published study that computes multiple orthogonal metric classes over one set of harmonization approaches and measures overlapping between the best approaches by these metrics.

Methods. We used the 2,234 approaches of the recent real-world benchmark on 7,174 B-cell lymphoma transcriptomes. They combine 31 harmonization methods, 14 batch-removal strategies, three imputations and two post-removal settings. We assigned its 87 polarity-adjusted scoring metrics, out of 344 registered, to five classes (global, local, distributional, structural and composite) and added three biology-facing classes: within-cohort marker correlation (L), cross-batch rank agreement of a marker panel (M), and leave-one-batch-out (LOBO) prediction of lymphoma subtypes and normal B cells (N). Each class resulted in top 112 approaches (5%), and we measured overlap by the Jaccard index.

Results. Harmonization methods of high harshness such as quantile normalization matched different batches to the same shape of distribution but were inferior by local and global metrics. Local and composite metrics resulted in the largest overlap of best approaches (Jaccard index 0.464, 71 shared). Classes L and M, M and N shared no approach, whereas L and N shared one: unharmonized output (01_raw) under the rare-batches-removed strategy. All three overlaps were below chance (hypergeometric p ≤ 0.020). The zero overlap of the 15 clustermap-selected approaches with class N was not (p = 0.46). Class N resulted in the ComBat family in 84 of its 112 places. Class M elected only 10_mnn and 12_scanorama, whose median marker correlations were −0.001 and −0.008.

Conclusions. Best transcriptomic harmonization approach is highly contingent on the quality metrics used. We provide a six-step recipe for metric classes, their thresholds and the cross-batch tests for selecting the best approaches.

### Introduction

Technical variation between sequencing platforms, RNA extraction protocols and tissue preservation methods separates samples of the same biological class, and it distorts inference whenever it is correlated with the outcome under study (Leek et al. 2010) . Algorithms that remove this variation from bulk expression matrices have been initially developed microarray integration (Lazar et al. 2013), whereas the cross-platform harmonization is more challenging, particularly, between microarrays and next-generation sequencing (NGS) (Borisov and Buzdin 2022) .

Each benchmark of these algorithms declares a different criterion of success. An early 2011 empirical comparison of cross-platform normalization scored methods on inter-platform concordance of gene lists supported by ROC-like curves and bootstrap (Rudy and Valafar 2011). A later comparison scored the methods by the accuracy of a downstream subtype classifier (Thompson et al. 2016). The single-cell integration literature supplied a third criterion, a trade-off between batch effect removal and biology conservation (Luecken et al. 2021). A benchmark of batch-effect correction methods for single-cell RNA sequencing recorded the same trade-off, reporting also that quality metrics can disagree with visual inspection of the embeddings (Tran et al. 2020). Two of the metrics that this literature contributed, the k-nearest-neighbor (KNN) batch-effect test (Büttner et al. 2018) and the local inverse Simpson’s index (Korsunsky et al. 2019), are now applied to bulk data, albeit there is still no standard of harmonization quality measurement (Borisov and Buzdin 2022).

The harmonization quality criteria are especially important in datasets where the biological contrast is small compared to the batch level technical differences. In the case of germinal center (GC) B-cell lymphomas, expression profiling splits diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) into germinal-center and activated B-cell types (Alizadeh et al. 2000) predicting survival after chemotherapy (Rosenwald et al. 2002). Survival in follicular lymphoma (FL) is predicted by subtle differences in immune-cell signatures of the microenvironment (Dave et al. 2004), and a 23-gene score separates FL patients into two outcome groups under immunochemotherapy (Huet et al. 2018). These small-scale transcriptomic differences are challenging to survive transfer between materials and platforms, explaining the cell-of-origin assignment reinvention for formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue (Scott et al. 2014) and multiple cohort sources for genetic subtypes of DLBCL (Chapuy et al. 2018; Schmitz et al. 2018). The same transfer problem governs expression signatures outside lymphoma, for example microsatellite instability status (Sorokin et al. 2021), therapy prescription in colorectal (Sorokin et al. 2022) and kidney cancer (Gudkov et al. 2022) and DNA repair pathway activation in thyroid tumors (Vladimirova et al. 2021). In a recent T-cell lymphoma xenograft study, principal-component analysis (PCA) showed only a partial overlap between primary tumors and their matched xenografts, which the authors attributed to tumor content and host cells, and that variation was removed by surrogate variable analysis (SVA) (Fiore et al. 2025).

In case of the real-world batch correction scenario, the analysis is complicated by confounding between the batch variable and with the class labels (Soneson et al. 2014) which drastically reduces predictors generalizability (Parker and Leek 2012). Moreover, cross-validation could be biased under these scenarios (Varma and Simon 2006) which is amplified by selection bias among thousands of genes in transcriptomic datasets containing only tens to hundreds of samples (Ambroise and McLachlan 2002). These issues lead to prediction failures of models deployed in clinical practice (Saeb et al. 2017; Whalen et al. 2021), described as the ‘reproducibility crisis’ in the machine learning science in general (Kapoor and Narayanan 2023) .

To address these current issues in transcriptomic cross-platform harmonization, we deeply analyzed the 2,234 harmonization approaches of a recently published multi-platform B-cell lymphoma benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17), which calculate 344 quality metrics. The approaches combine 31 methods, 14 prior batch-removal strategies, three imputation schemes and two post-removal settings. For each metric class among local, global, distributional, composite, biomarker genes correlation before and after harmonization (class L), cross-batch agreement of biomarker genes (class M) and leave-one-batch-out (LOBO) prediction metrics (class N), we extensively compared harmonization methods and batch removal strategies, selecting 5% best approaches by a given metric class. The metric classes favored different harmonization methods: the global class placed 84 of its 112 best approaches under 03_limma and 13_fsmvn, whereas the local class was led by 10_mnn with 19 approaches. Class L placed 93 of its 112 best approaches under the unharmonized output 01_raw and the minimally changed 36_explobatch, so it rewarded the approaches that change expression least. Batch PCReg separated methods more strongly than batch-removal strategies (Kruskal-Wallis effect size ε² = 0.739 against 0.100), with the RNA-seq-only strategy highest on batch PCReg and the FFPE-only strategy least often passing the joint class L and M quality gate. Local and composite metrics resulted in the largest overlap (Jaccard index 0.464, 71 shared approaches). Classes L and M, M and N - shared no approach, whereas L and N shared one approach, the unharmonized output 01_raw under the rare-batches-removed strategy with softimpute imputation and post-removal. The 15 approaches selected by the published clustermap shared none of the 112 that class N elected. Class N resulted in the ComBat family (05_combat, 29_combat_ref, 06_combat_seq and 08_inmoose_combatseq) in 84 of its 112 places. Class M elected two methods, 10_mnn and 12_scanorama, whose median marker correlation was −0.001 and −0.008.

Finally, we propose a novel six step framework for harmonization quality metrics usage to select best harmonization approaches in other bulk transcriptomic datasets, including the non-B cell lymphoma ones.

### Materials and methods

#### Dataset, harmonization approaches and the analysis set

The GC B-cell lymphomas dataset, the 14 prior batch-removal strategies, the three imputation schemes, the harmonization method registry and the optional post-removal step are specified in the benchmarking study previously published by our team (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17): 7,174 bulk transcriptomes of germinal-center B-cell lymphomas, pooled from 88 cohorts profiled by RNA sequencing or by Affymetrix, Illumina and Agilent microarrays. We re-used that output and computed no new harmonization. The most important algorithms were ComBat with its reference (Combat-ref) and count-based (Combat-seq) variants (Johnson et al. 2007; Zhang et al. 2020), surrogate variable analysis (Leek and Storey 2007), mutual nearest neighbors (Haghverdi et al. 2018), feature-specific quantile normalization (Franks et al. 2018), cross-platform normalization by XPN (Shabalin et al. 2008) and Shambhala-2 (Borisov et al. 2019; Borisov et al. 2022); the full registry with the parameters used is given in the source benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17).

A harmonization approach is defined as one combination of batch-removal strategy, imputation, harmonization method and post-removal. We defined the approaches set by the condition that fewer than 5% of samples carry no defined expression value for all genes (pct_samples_allNA < 5). This set contained 2,234 approaches over 31 methods, 14 strategies, three imputations and both post-removal settings. Two methods, 34_arsyn and 38_harman, had a minimum of 74.23% of samples with no defined expression across all 84 of their approaches and were not included into the final approach set. Shambhala-2 was implemented using its standard P0 and Q0 calibration datasets and was named as 20_shambhala in the benchmark. The metric snapshots store the same method under its parameter-variant name, shambhala_P0std_Q0std, and the two names agree on 78 of 79 numeric metric columns over their 84 shared approaches, the exception being compute time. We therefore renamed the variant to 20_shambhala on loading; treating the two names as different methods drops these 84 approaches and reduces the analysis set to 2,150.

#### Metric classes and the best approaches selection procedure

Of the 344 quality metrics registered by the source benchmark (Supplementary File 2 of the source benchmark), 87 carry a scoring polarity and are used for every ranking below, and the remaining 257 are descriptive. Of the 87 scoring metrics, 51 have positive and 36 negative polarity, meaning that a higher or a lower value, respectively, is better. We assigned the scoring metrics of the source benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17) to four classes by their column prefix: global, covering the 11 metrics of principal component regression (PCReg) and the dispersion separability criterion (DSC); local, covering the KNN batch-effect test (kBET), the integration and cell-type local inverse Simpson’s indices (iLISI and cLISI), average silhouette width, the cell-specific mixing score and graph connectivity; distributional, covering the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistics and the per-gene coefficient of variation of batch means; and structural, covering tSNE and UMAP embedding entropy and centroid dispersion, the distance ratio and the Watermelon score (Supplementary File 1, sheet Metric_class_assignment). The per-component R² and variance-explained columns carry no polarity, so they are descriptive and enter no class, and the seven scoring metrics that describe missing values and bimodality are likewise left unassigned. A fifth class, the composite, is the equally weighted mean of those four classes.

Within a class we converted every metric to a rank percentile over the 2,234 approaches, oriented so that a higher percentile is better according to the polarity assignments from the benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17), and averaged the percentiles into one class score per approach (Supplementary File 1, sheet Class_percentile_scores). For each class we took top 5% approaches as the best ones (112 approaches in total). For comparison, we treated the 15 approaches selected by the published clustermap (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17) as one more set. Overlap between each two elected sets was calculated as Jaccard index.

We also built a separate generalizability and biology preservation index as the mean of six rank percentiles: the full and multiclass-only LOBO three-class macro F1, the two-class LOBO macro F1, the mean within-cohort marker correlation before and after harmonization, the class M same-minus-different-biology margin of biomarker genes subtracted by its unharmonized baseline, and the worst multiclass fold. Macro F1 was taken for both LOBO targets so that the prediction components of the index measure the same quantity. The worst multiclass fold (f1_mc_min) is the minimum, over the held-out batches that contain at least two biological classes, of the three-class macro F1 of an approach. We compared batch-removal strategies against each other, imputations against each other and harmonization methods against each other, and the clustermap-selected group from the benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17) was treated as an isolated named group.

#### The biology evaluating metric classes L, M and N

Since gene signatures were systematically shown to predict biological subtypes better than individual genes (Jovčevska et al. 2019; Sorokin et al. 2020) , for classes L and M, we took 633-gene marker panel assembled from published FL and DLBCL studies and its narrow 56-gene versions as described in the benchmarking study (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17); the coverage of each marker gene across the harmonization attempts is shown in Supplementary Figure 1A. In the L class, the Spearman correlation of these gene’s expression before and after harmonization, together with the contrast against a housekeeping control (Risso et al. 2014), were calculated, and the distribution of the per-gene correlation within each signature is shown in Supplementary Figure 1C. We treated a cohort wise mean marker correlation before and after harmonization above 0.75 as a necessary, albeit not sufficient, condition for good quality harmonization approach.

For class M metric, we calculated Spearman rank correlation of the marker panel between pairs of batches, computed separately for pairs of the same biological group and for pairs of different groups (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17). The margin was calculated as the same-biology agreement subtracted by the different-biology agreement. We also analyzed the margin relative to the unharmonized baseline expression matrices at the same strategy and imputation, so we were able to extract approaches that increased same-biology agreement and decreased different-biology agreement against their own baseline.

Class N metrics were designed as leave-one-batch-out prediction to measure a transferability and generalizability of the best harmonization approaches selected. For each approach and each of the 24 RNA batches we excluded this batch and fitted logistic regression in principal components on the training batches alone for the three-class target (DLBCL, FL and normal B cells) and for the two-class target (FL against DLBCL). Then we projected the PCA coordinates at the held-out batch and predicted its biology labels using the previously fitted logistic regression, calculating Area Under Curve (AUC) and F1 scores. We also controlled this calculation using 100 biological label permutations and averaged all the N class metrics separately on multiclass batches (having more than one biology class) and on all batches. Multiclass-only batches were selected in order to remove a potential F1/AUC bias in batches that contain single biology group. The AUC is undefined on a held-out batch with a single biology group, so every AUC mean is a mean over multiclass batches only.

#### Statistics and software

Group comparisons across three or more groups used the Kruskal-Wallis H test, with ε² as its effect size, and monotone associations used the Spearman correlation coefficient. Two groups were compared by the two-sided Mann-Whitney U test, and matched pairs of approaches, which share strategy, method and the remaining pipeline settings and differ in one factor only, by the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Proportions were compared between two groups by the two-sided Fisher's exact test and against their distribution in the analysis set by the chi-square goodness-of-fit test or the two-sided binomial test. The number of strategies in which one harshness tier ranked first was compared with the one-in-three chance level by the two-sided binomial test, and a count of strategies with a lower value on one principal component than on the next by the two-sided binomial sign test. The overlap of two elected sets was compared with the overlap expected for two random sets of the same sizes by the hypergeometric distribution, reported as the probability of an overlap at least as large, or at least as small, as observed. All p-values are raw, without correction for multiple testing. Per-strategy coefficients were analyzed as their ranges and medians and selected groups were reported by their medians and median absolute deviations (MAD); the spread of group means is reported as their range, the maximum minus the minimum. We ran the analyses under Python 3.11 with pandas 2.3.3, numpy 1.26.4, scipy 1.17.1, scikit-learn 1.8.0, statsmodels 0.14.6, matplotlib 3.10.8 and seaborn 0.13.2.

#### AI usage

Claude Code with the Claude Opus 5 model was used for literature retrieval, analysis code and language checks, in a plan-then-act mode: the agent wrote an implementation plan, the authors reviewed and corrected it over several cycles, and only then were the code and documents written. ### Results and discussion

#### Metric classes capture non-overlapping components of harmonization quality

We started the analysis from the two metric families that dominate the published comparisons, the global PCA-based and the local neighborhood and investigated their order in the canonical set of 2,234 approaches. As the global representative we took PCReg in polarity-adjusted form: 1 means that the annotation column explains none of the PCA variation and 0 means that it explains all of it. Plotting PCReg for the biology column against PCReg for the batch column gave a weak negative association across the whole set (Spearman ρ = −0.159, p = 3.7 × 10⁻¹⁴, Figure 1A and 1B for harmonization methods and batch removal strategies, respectively). The clustermap-selected approaches had a higher batch PCReg than the remaining approaches, with a median of 0.871 against 0.626 (Mann-Whitney p = 6.3 × 10⁻³). Their biology PCReg did not differ from that of the remaining approaches, with a median of 0.820 against 0.802 (p = 0.69), so the clustermap group stood apart from the other approaches along the batch axis of the plot only. The MADs of both comparisons are given in Supplementary File 1, sheet Paired_tests.

Individual methods behaved in divergent and counterintuitive manner. The batch PCReg of 33_amdbnorm was exactly 1.000 in each of its 14 completed runs. The method 13_fsmvn reached a median of 1.000 with minimum 0.997, but both methods were selected as the clustermap best approaches only for the no-removal strategy (Figure 1A). The methods 05_combat and 29_combat_ref separated biology better than the remaining methods across the A, B, I, J and E1 to E3 strategies (median biology PCReg 0.568 against 0.778, Mann-Whitney p = 1.3 × 10⁻²⁷, Figure 1A) without being selected by the clustermap. Their biology PCReg spanned 0.357 to 0.823 over these strategies. By PCA batch correction, 14_qsmooth was the last method, with a median of 0.208 against 0.672 for the other methods (p = 1.3 × 10⁻²⁸; its MAD is given in Supplementary File 1, sheet Paired_tests). Among strategies, malignant-only, FFPE-only and no-removal were weakest on biology, with medians of 0.939, 0.925 and 0.883, respectively. Each of the three was higher than the other strategies (Mann-Whitney p ≤ 6.4 × 10⁻²⁷). RNA-seq-only was strongest on batch, with a median of 0.872 against 0.563 for the other strategies (p = 4.5 × 10⁻²⁰, Figure 1B). The malignant-only and FFPE-only strategies contain no normal B cells, so their biology column contains only four classes (DLBCL, FL, Burkitt lymphoma and High grade B-cell lymphoma) potentially explaining their high PCReg by biology.

Decomposing the variance by principal component showed the batch signal transformation by harmonization methods (Figure 1C; Supplementary Figure 2A, B). Five methods (15_fsqn_py, 17_quantile, 18_rank, 20_shambhala and 25_angel) spread the variance slowly across components, placing 17.3 to 25.3% of it on the first component. On the second component the same five methods placed 11.3 to 15.3%. Eight further methods and the clustermap best group concentrated 26.2 to 43.3% on the first component (Figure 1C). The remaining 18 methods placed up to 79.9% there, with the maximum for 11_harmony and 79.5% for 27_dwd. For the five leading methods by mean batch PCReg (03_limma, 07_pycombat, 13_fsmvn, 28_npn and 33_amdbnorm) the batch-explained R² stayed below 1% at every one of the first ten components, with a maximum of 0.002 at the second component of 07_pycombat (Supplementary Figure 2B). Without harmonization and without batch removal the same dataset gives a batch PCReg of 0.226, which means that batch accounts for 77.4% of the PCA variance summed over all components.

The local metrics ordered the approaches in a different manner compared to the global metrics (Figure 2). Taking the local inverse Simpson’s index as a main local metric, 10_mnn led on batch mixing with a median iLISI of 1.72 against 1.16 for the remaining approaches (Mann-Whitney p = 3.3 × 10⁻⁴⁰). Last came 14_qsmooth, at a median of 1.07 (p = 8.0 × 10⁻²⁷ against the rest, Figure 2A). The MADs of both methods are given in Supplementary File 1, sheet Paired_tests. Batch and biology mixing increased together (Spearman ρ = 0.527, p = 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁶⁰, Figure 2A). Colored by strategy, the association held inside every strategy except the Affymetrix-only one, with ρ between 0.374 and 0.815 (each p ≤ 9.2 × 10⁻⁷, Figure 2B; Supplementary File 1, sheet Scatter_correlations). Inside the Affymetrix-only strategy it was not significant (ρ = 0.147, p = 0.069). The tSNE entropy of the batch labels agreed with that ordering, with a median of 0.179 for 10_mnn against 0.018 for 14_qsmooth (Mann-Whitney p = 2.3 × 10⁻²⁸, Figure 2C; the MADs are given in Supplementary File 1, sheet Paired_tests). In contrast, tSNE centroid dispersion, a global quantity computed in the same tSNE embedding, split the methods into a low group and a high group, with method means between 0.24 for 13_fsmvn and 1.00 for 27_dwd, and placed the clustermap group on the boundary between them (Figure 2D).

The initial screening of harmonization approaches by global, local and distributional metrics showed that the three families ordered the same set of approaches differently. In the next chapters we deeply investigated these differences in order to derive a unified framework of harmonization approaches benchmarking.

#### Global variance-based metrics saturate below the threshold of biological preservation

Twelve of the 31 methods overlapped batches better than biology in PCA space, and five of them (03_limma, 07_pycombat, 13_fsmvn, 28_npn and 33_amdbnorm) reached a mean batch PCReg at least 0.999, with 33_amdbnorm at exactly 1.000 (Supplementary Figure 3A). At strategy level only two of the 14 strategies, RNA-seq-only and Affymetrix-only, overlapped batches better than biology (Supplementary Figure 3B). Batch PCReg differed across methods far more than across strategies (Kruskal-Wallis ε² = 0.739 against 0.100, both p ≤ 6.7 × 10⁻⁴³), which places the choice of method above the choice of strategy at this level of batch effect quality control.

Looking at the PCReg-saturated approaches, we examined the 198 approaches whose batch PCReg exceeded 0.9999, which were generated by six methods (03_limma, 13_fsmvn, 07_pycombat, 21_harmonizr, 33_amdbnorm and 28_npn). Of these, 98.5% returned a kBET acceptance rate under 0.1 (Supplementary Figure 4E, F). Over all approaches the same share was 97.0%, and the saturated approaches did not differ from the remaining ones (96.9%; Fisher's exact p = 0.27). PCReg saturation therefore does not indicate poorer local batch mixing than almost every approach of the benchmark already shows.

The remaining global metrics closely reproduced PCReg and added little separation. We first compared PCReg for the batch column with PCReg for the coarser platform and cohort columns (Supplementary Figure 5A, B). Because one platform can correspond to two or three batches, PCReg by platform followed PCReg by batch almost linearly (Spearman ρ = 0.974, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰, Supplementary Figure 5A), with the FFPE-only and FF-only strategies closest to the diagonal, where each platform keeps a single batch. PCReg by cohort followed PCReg by batch less closely (ρ = 0.606, p = 2.1 × 10⁻²²⁴), and most approaches retained more cohort-related than batch-related variance (Supplementary Figure 5B). DSC for the batch column followed PCReg along a monotone sigmoid (ρ = −0.970, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰, Supplementary Figure 5C). DSC for the platform column followed DSC for the batch column linearly on a logarithmic scale (ρ = 0.966, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰, Supplementary Figure 5D). DSC for the cohort column, in contrast, formed two clouds of points that the strategies did not explain and that more plausibly reflect the harmonization method (ρ = 0.544 against batch DSC, p = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁷², Supplementary Figure 5E). All 15 clustermap approaches were below the overall median batch DSC of 0.962 and lower than the remaining approaches (Mann-Whitney p = 0.011). The four MNN approaches of the group lay at the boundary (0.781 to 0.957). The FSQN R approaches lay about two orders of magnitude lower (0.001 to 0.010). Such behavior can be explained by the definition of DSC as between-group scatter divided by within-group scatter, which is sensitive to outliers. The tSNE centroid dispersion by batch decreased with batch PCReg along an arc (ρ = −0.864, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰, Supplementary Figure 5F, G). A subgroup of 11_harmony approaches under the FF-only and FFPE-only strategies lay below this trend, although their dispersion did not differ from that of the other methods within either strategy (Mann–Whitney p = 0.32 and 0.15, respectively). Linking each approach with and without post-removal showed that post-removal raised batch PCReg under every one of the 14 strategies (Wilcoxon signed-rank, p ≤ 4.3 × 10⁻⁶ for each; Supplementary Figure 5H). The shift was largest in the Affymetrix-only strategy, with a median of 0.075 against 0.007 to 0.028 elsewhere, presumably because post-removal drops the already quantile-normalized SOM cohort, which makes up a large share of this strategy. Average silhouette width tracked PCReg in a noisier manner (ρ = −0.630, p = 1.9 × 10⁻²⁴⁷, Supplementary Figure 5I, J). The Affymetrix-only and FFPE-only strategies were worst, with means of 0.165 and 0.077, each above the other strategies (Mann-Whitney p ≤ 3.8 × 10⁻⁷, Supplementary Figure 5I). The best methods were 02_median_scaling and 33_amdbnorm, with means of −0.286 and −0.276 (p ≤ 1.3 × 10⁻⁸ against the rest, Supplementary Figure 5J).

The two pipeline factors, namely imputation and post-removal, impacted the harmonization performance to the least extent according to the source benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17), and we quantified them here on the global metric. We compared the imputations over the 11 methods with the lowest batch PCReg (Supplementary Figure 6A). Softimpute exceeded KNN imputation in these methods by between 1.7 and 9.7 percentage points, with a mean of 4.8. In matched pairs of approaches, which share strategy, method and post-removal, the median gain of softimpute over KNN imputation was 0.042 in batch PCReg (Wilcoxon signed-rank p = 1.5 × 10⁻²⁸). Strict exclusion scored below KNN imputation in the same matched comparison, by a median of 0.020 (p = 2.7 × 10⁻²²). Over the 11 methods, the means were 0.375 for strict exclusion against 0.394 for KNN imputation. Post-removal raised the mean batch PCReg for 30 of the 31 methods. Over matched pairs of approaches the median shift was 0.011 (Wilcoxon signed-rank p = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴⁰). The largest increase was for 22_tmm, from 0.70 up to 0.88. Next came 23_vst, which increased from 0.70 to 0.83. The single method with PCReg decrease was 12_scanorama, from 0.49 to 0.48 (Supplementary Figure 6C).

Batch removal strategies impacted batch PCReg to higher degree compared to imputations and post removals (Kruskal–Wallis ε² = 0.100 across strategies against 0.002 across imputations and 0.002 across post-removal settings). Every method reached at least 0.76 under the RNA-seq-only strategy, 0.72 under Affymetrix-only and 0.46 under Affymetrix-extended (Supplementary Figure 6B). In contrast, the 16_fsqn_r method decreased from its cross-strategy mean of 0.83 to 0.30 under microarray-only and 0.08 under FFPE-only. Under no removal the same method reached a median of 0.995, and both strategies fell below it (Mann-Whitney p = 2.2 × 10⁻³ for each), proving that feature-specific quantile normalization performs poorly in cross-platform FFPE-only dataset.

As defined above, a PCReg of 0 is the worst scenario for the batch columns (RNA_BATCH, PLATFORM_RNA, COHORT_LABEL, RNASEQ_SOURCE) and the best one for the biology columns (Major_Group, Diagnosis_cell_type_unified, TUMOR_NORMAL). Because the variance is unevenly distributed across the PCs, we compared PCReg RNA_BATCH against R² of RNA_BATCH in the first PC by harmonization methods and batch removal strategies (Supplementary Figure 6D, 6E, respectively). As expected, we observed a strong negative relationship between the two (Spearman ρ = −0.939, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰), with the clustermap best approaches occupying the lower right corner of the plot. 10_mnn, 20_shambhala and 16_fsqn_r were shifted above the main diagonal, suggesting that their batch-explained variance is moved towards lower rank PCs. The same was true for the RNA-seq-only strategy (Supplementary Figure 6E).

For all the batch removal strategies we also investigated batch-explained variance for the first 10 PCs (Supplementary Figure 6F; Supplementary Figure 2C, D), examining the percentage of variance explained and the R² for RNA_BATCH at each PC. The joint group of the clustermap best approaches ranked second by batch PCReg after the RNA-seq-only strategy (shown earlier in Supplementary Figure 3B). Both the RNA-seq-only strategy and the clustermap best approaches group showed the lowest average percentage of variance explained by the first PC (30.4 and 37.9%, respectively), and the smoothest decay of the percentage by PC number (Supplementary Figure 2C). The RNA-seq-only value was lower than over the other strategies (Mann-Whitney p = 4.7 × 10⁻³⁶). The clustermap group value was lower than over the remaining approaches (p = 0.038). The Affymetrix-only strategy showed the highest average percentage, 66.0% (p = 6.1 × 10⁻¹⁶ against the other strategies), presumably because the already quantile-normalized SOM cohort makes up a larger share of the samples in this strategy than in any other. The decay trajectory of the RNA_BATCH R² for the RNA-seq-only strategy and the best approaches started at 0.29 and 0.23, respectively, whereas the other strategies, except the Affymetrix-only and Affymetrix-extended ones, had higher first-PC values (Supplementary Figure 2D). Of the 14 batch removal strategies, 10 had a lower RNA_BATCH R² for the first PC than for the second, a count that does not exceed the equal-odds expectation (binomial sign test p = 0.18).

Taking together, global metrics in-depth analysis showed that global metrics form a weak, permissive criterion of harmonization quality. Both biology and batch PCReg should be measured, and other metric classes should be added to evaluate harmonization quality robustly.

#### Batch-associated variance is redistributed across principal components instead of being eliminated

We separately investigated distribution of batch-associated variance over principal components by harmonization methods and batch removal strategies (Supplementary Figure 2A–D). Ten of the 14 strategies, and 19 of the 31 methods, placed a lower batch-explained R² on the first principal component than on some later one (Supplementary Figure 2B, D). Among the five methods whose explained variance decayed most slowly, four (excluding 20_shambhala) reached their maximum batch R² away from the first component (Supplementary Figure 2B). On the first component, batch explained 22.9% of the variance in the clustermap group, 64.8% in the unharmonized data and 90.8% under 14_qsmooth, the weakest method on the PCA metrics (Supplementary Figure 2B). The first component itself carried 37.9%, 73.5% and 53.7% of the total variance in the same three groups (Supplementary Figure 2A). On the second component the batch R² was 15.2%, 65.9% and 67.1%, respectively, so the unharmonized data and 14_qsmooth kept most of their batch signal beyond the first component. The second component itself carried 11.6%, 9.7% and 13.5% of the total variance in the same three groups. The individual principal components analysis therefore showed that harmonization benchmarking based on the first component only inherently underestimates batch effect, and harmonization methods can hide the batch-related variance into the lower rank principal components which should be accounted for during benchmarking.

#### Local neighborhood metrics discriminate well between good and bad approaches but are vulnerable to batch composition

Only six of the 31 methods (02_median_scaling, 10_mnn, 16_fsqn_r, 25_angel, 28_npn and 33_amdbnorm) mixed batches better than biology by LISI (Supplementary Figure 7A). The kBET acceptance rate behaved as more stringent criterion: it stayed under 0.01 for 82.0% of the 2,234 approaches. It stayed under 0.10 for 97.0% of them (Supplementary Figure 8A). Five clustermap best approaches exceeded 0.10, against 2.8% of the remaining approaches (Fisher's exact p = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵), and all five were SVA approaches under the RNA-seq-only and FFPE-only strategies. By mean, eight methods exceeded the batch kBET acceptance rate of the unharmonized baseline, 0.027, and only two of them did so by a wide margin, 22_tmm at 0.331 and 23_vst at 0.230, both run under the RNA-seq-only strategy alone (Supplementary Figure 8C). The baseline, however, is zero in the median and its mean is carried by a minority of approaches, so the rank comparison gives a different picture. By ranks, 14 methods exceeded the baseline and three fell below it (two-sided Mann-Whitney p < 0.05 each; Supplementary File 1, sheet Paired_tests). The strongest rank gain over the baseline was for 10_mnn, 25_angel and 16_fsqn_r (p ≤ 8.3 × 10⁻¹⁵). In contrast, 23_vst did not differ from the baseline (p = 0.30).

According to their design, local values depended on how many classes the strategy leaves in the data, which limits comparison across strategies. The malignant-only and FFPE-only strategies retained four malignant groups and returned the lowest biology LISI (medians 1.10 and 1.07). The FF-only strategy preserved all the normal B-cell types and returned the highest value (median 1.62). Each of the three strategies differed from the other strategies (Mann-Whitney p ≤ 2.8 × 10⁻⁷⁶, Supplementary Figure 7B). The same two strategies returned the lowest biology graph connectivity, with means of 0.727 for FFPE-only and 0.646 for malignant-only (each lower than the other strategies, Mann-Whitney p ≤ 6.0 × 10⁻²⁴, Supplementary Figure 9D), although fewer biology groups are expected to increase connectivity, which could derive from their assembly from heterogeneous FFPE batches. Colored by method, graph connectivity by biology and by platform were nearly unrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.080, p = 4.3 × 10⁻⁴, Supplementary Figure 9C). In that plane 10_mnn, 25_angel, 09_ruv and 16_fsqn_r lay in the upper right corner near the clustermap best approaches, and 02_median_scaling, 07_pycombat, 03_limma, 04_sva and 11_harmony in the lower left corner, with both lower biology preservation and a more fragmented platform-level structure (Supplementary Figure 9C). The RNA-seq-only strategy produced a tight group of 41 approaches with kBET acceptance rates between 0.654 and 0.867 (Supplementary Figure 8B). These approaches came from 18 methods, and none of them was selected by the clustermap, because their LISI values stayed between 1.24 and 1.38.

We then visualized batch and biology LISI on heatmaps by harmonization method and batch removal strategy (Supplementary Figure 7C, D). The clustermap best approaches occupied local peaks of batch LISI (Supplementary Figure 7C) but not the corresponding local minima of biology LISI (Supplementary Figure 7D). Against the other approaches, the clustermap group had a higher batch LISI (Mann–Whitney p = 2.0 × 10⁻⁷). Its biology LISI was indistinguishable from theirs (p = 0.80), which reflects how small the biological differences are relative to the technical ones in this dataset. By strategy, the RNA-seq-only strategy had the highest batch kBET acceptance rate, followed by the two Affymetrix strategies and the FF-only strategy, whereas removing bad or rare batches, or keeping malignant samples only, left kBET near zero (Supplementary Figure 8D; Kruskal–Wallis across strategies p = 1.2 × 10⁻⁷⁸).

We also compared LISI with the embedding-based mixing metrics. The tSNE entropy of the batch labels followed batch LISI closely (Spearman ρ = 0.915, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰), and 10_mnn together with the three clustermap 04_sva approaches formed a separate linear trend with a higher entropy for a given LISI (Supplementary Figure 8E). By strategy the differences were smaller, and the no-removal, rare-batches-removed, Affymetrix-extended and microarray-only strategies lay on the lower side of the trend (Supplementary Figure 8F). tSNE centroid dispersion decreased as tSNE entropy rose (ρ = −0.493, p = 5.6 × 10⁻¹³⁷, Supplementary Figure 8G, H). 10_mnn, with a minority of the 04_sva, 11_harmony, 12_scanorama and 16_fsqn_r approaches, formed a diagonal cloud of high entropy, whereas the other methods formed two dense, round clouds (Supplementary Figure 8G). The strategies formed layers present in both clouds (Supplementary Figure 8H), so a given method can produce either good or poor batch overlap in the embedding, and the composition of the batches governs how well they mix locally. UMAP and tSNE entropy agreed almost exactly (ρ = 0.992, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰) and added no difference between methods or strategies (Supplementary Figure 8I, J). UMAP and tSNE centroid dispersion were also strongly related (ρ = 0.814, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰), with the same two clouds of methods (Supplementary Figure 8K) and each strategy present in both (Supplementary Figure 8L). The clustermap best approaches were spread along this trend line, so the selection gave priority to local mixing over group separation in both non-linear embeddings.

Finally, we compared LISI and kBET against PCReg (Supplementary Figure 4). Batch LISI rose with batch PCReg (Spearman ρ = 0.557, p = 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁸²), with 10_mnn, 04_sva, 11_harmony, 12_scanorama and 16_fsqn_r above the general trend (Supplementary Figure 4A). By strategy the approaches formed layers similar to, but noisier than, those of tSNE centroid dispersion against tSNE entropy (Supplementary Figure 4B). For the biology column the relationship was negative (ρ = −0.403, p = 4.0 × 10⁻⁸⁸), and the clustermap best approaches were spread uniformly over the plane (Supplementary Figure 4C). Strategies formed horizontal layers of variable PCReg and fixed biology LISI (Supplementary Figure 4D), the malignant-only and FFPE-only layers at the lowest LISI and the FF-only layer at the highest, in line with the number of biological classes each strategy keeps. kBET acceptance rate rose weakly with batch PCReg (ρ = 0.396, p = 1.1 × 10⁻⁸⁴, Supplementary Figure 4E), and only 04_sva and 10_mnn had a kBET above 0.1 in a sizable number of their approaches. By strategy, the RNA-seq-only approaches formed a compact group of high kBET and high PCReg (Supplementary Figure 4F), none of which was selected by the clustermap.

Taking together, the in-depth analysis of local metrics showed that reaching the maximum of one local metric is not sufficient for the clustermap best selection. Moreover, the local metrics were highly dependent on prior batch removal strategies. These findings indicate that a harmonization quality benchmarking pipeline should treat local metrics as necessary but not sufficient metric family.

#### Distributional convergence is the only axis on which the high harshness harmonization methods rank first

We further deeply compared metrics of distributional similarity between harmonization approaches. On the fraction of cohort pairs whose expression distributions differ by the KS test, 29 of the 31 methods had a mean score below the unharmonized baseline of 0.948 (Supplementary Figure 9A). By ranks, 26 methods had lower values than the baseline (two-sided Mann-Whitney p < 0.05 each), and 04_sva, 31_ruv3prps and 36_explobatch did not differ from it (Supplementary File 1, sheet Paired_tests). Only 27_dwd was worse than the raw expression matrix, at 0.965, a value that did not differ from it (p = 0.46). Only ten methods brought the mean below 0.7, ranging from 0.173 for 28_npn to 0.691 for 03_limma. The second lowest was 26_xpn, at 0.217. The clustermap best group reached a mean of 0.560, lower than the remaining approaches (Mann-Whitney p = 9.6 × 10⁻³), so 56.0% of cohort pairs still differed after the best harmonization by the clustermap assessment. By strategy, the no-removal strategy had the lowest KS fraction significant, followed by the clustermap group and the RNA-seq-only strategy (no removal against the other strategies, Mann–Whitney p = 0.0033; Supplementary Figure 9B). Distance ratio separated the clustermap group as well, with a median batch distance ratio of 0.914 against 0.757 for the remaining approaches (Mann-Whitney p = 1.0 × 10⁻³, Supplementary Figure 9E). Only the two methods 27_dwd (0.508) and 14_qsmooth (0.531) had the metric below the unharmonized dataset (0.566). The batch and biology distance ratios were nearly unrelated (Spearman ρ = −0.074, p = 4.8 × 10⁻⁴). By strategy, the malignant-only, no-removal and FFPE-only strategies had the highest biology distance ratio, that is, the weakest biology preservation (Kruskal–Wallis across strategies p = 5.8 × 10⁻¹⁶¹; Supplementary Figure 9F).

We then analyzed the Watermelon score, which measures how well an approach separates classes on a clustermap in gene expression space, by batch and by biology columns (Supplementary Figure 9G–J). On the batch column the clustermap group had a lower Watermelon score than the remaining approaches, a median of 0.318 against 0.352 (Mann-Whitney p = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴, Supplementary Figure 9G). On the biology column it did not differ from them (0.290 against 0.306, p = 0.086). The batch and biology scores were moderately related (Spearman ρ = 0.422, p = 5.0 × 10⁻⁹⁷, Supplementary Figure 9G). The lowest batch score belonged to 10_mnn and three RNA-seq-only 11_harmony approaches (Supplementary Figure 9G). By strategy, the Affymetrix-only strategy scored worst on the batch column, and the malignant-only and FFPE-only strategies, with four malignant classes each, scored best on the biology column (Supplementary Figure 9H). Averaged over all batch columns, the clustermap group again scored lower than the remaining approaches (median 0.311 against 0.368, p = 1.4 × 10⁻⁵, Supplementary Figure 9I). Averaged over the biology columns, the clustermap group did not differ from them (median 0.350 against 0.372, p = 0.21). The two averages were weakly related (ρ = 0.124, p = 3.6 × 10⁻⁹, Supplementary Figure 9I), 10_mnn formed a compact group of low batch scores (Supplementary Figure 9I), and the strategies separated into compact groups, the malignant-only approaches lowest on the biology average and the FFPE-only approaches highest, although both keep the same four malignant classes (Supplementary Figure 9J). Output expression quantiles were similar across strategies, except for the Affymetrix-only strategy, which had a larger per-gene standard deviation than the other strategies (median 3.60 against 2.75, Mann–Whitney p = 6.8 × 10⁻⁶; Supplementary Figure 9K). Its maximum expression, by contrast, did not differ from the other strategies (p = 0.30). Because the Watermelon score separated the clustermap group on the batch columns only, its biology component captures a mode of harmonization quality that the clustermap selection did not reward, which makes this metric a candidate for an independent metric class to be compared to the global and local families.

We then compared methods by their harshness score defined in the harmonization benchmark (Nikitin et al. 2026 Sep 17) as low harshness is linear transformation, high harshness is significant distribution reshaping (for example in quantile normalization) and medium harshness is everything in between. There were 344 low, 1,042 medium and 848 high harshness approaches for the comparison. Within each strategy we identified the tier with the highest or the lowest mean, and compared the number of strategies in which the same tier ranked first with the one-in-three chance level by the two-sided binomial test. Low-harshness methods had the highest mean batch PCReg in 11 of the 14 strategies (binomial p = 6.9 × 10⁻⁴, Supplementary Figure 10A). They also had the highest mean batch LISI in 10 of 14 (p = 4.0 × 10⁻³, Supplementary Figure 10D). High-harshness methods had the lowest tSNE entropy in 12 of 14 (p = 8.2 × 10⁻⁵, Supplementary Figure 10C). High harshness prevailed over low and middle harshness on one axis only, namely distributional convergence, where it demonstrated the lowest KS fraction significant in 13 of the 14 strategies (p = 6.1 × 10⁻⁶, Supplementary Figure 10F). Inside any single strategy, the tier difference reached significance in at most two of the 14 strategies for each of these metrics (Kruskal-Wallis p < 0.05), so the finding rests on the consistent direction across strategies, while the difference inside one strategy is small. On the biology columns, low-harshness methods had the highest biology LISI, that is, the weakest local preservation of biology, in 12 of the 14 strategies (binomial test against the one-in-three chance level, p = 8.2 × 10⁻⁵; Supplementary Figure 10E), so their stronger local mixing of batches came with weaker local preservation of biology. On biology PCReg the low tier was lowest in 7 of the 14 strategies, which does not exceed chance (p = 0.25; Supplementary Figure 10B). Matching expression distributions to a same distribution is a distinguishing feature of harshest transformations, but it does not lead to improvement of local or global metrics.

We also calculated and compared a composite score, which was the equally weighted composite of all 87 metrics. Expectedly, it ranked the clustermap group as first at a mean of 0.602, above the remaining approaches (Mann-Whitney p = 1.8 × 10⁻⁸, Supplementary Figure 11A). The RNA-seq-only strategy was next, at a mean of 0.578 (p = 1.6 × 10⁻⁵⁵ against the other strategies). To find which metrics drive the composite, we split the score of each approach into the contributions of each metric type, metric group and annotation column, and measured the spread of each contribution as the range of its group means (maximum minus minimum), counting the 15 clustermap-selected approaches as one more group (Supplementary File 1, sheet Composite_dispersion). By metric type of the source benchmark, global distance metrics supplied 47 of the 87 columns and 0.360 of the clustermap group’s score (Supplementary Figure 11A, B). Their contribution ranged by 0.066 across strategies and by 0.085 across methods. Local neighborhood metrics supplied only 22 columns and 0.109 of the score. Their contribution nonetheless ranged by 0.071 across strategies and by 0.075 across methods, a spread close to that of the global distance metrics from less than a third of their contribution. By metric group, the PCA-based variance decomposition metrics (PCReg and DSC) ranged by only 0.017 across strategies and 0.030 across methods (Supplementary Figure 11C, D). The neighbor-based integration metrics ranged by 0.043 across strategies and 0.049 across methods. By annotation column, batch columns supplied 57 of the 87 metrics and 0.403 of the clustermap group’s score (Supplementary Figure 11E, F). The 18 biology columns separated approaches less than the batch columns did: their contribution ranged by 0.053 across strategies, against 0.121 for the batch columns (Supplementary Figure 11E). Across methods the two ranges were 0.041 and 0.154 (Supplementary Figure 11F).

We also sorted all approaches by the composite score and revealed an upper tail of 150 approaches above 0.6, which is 6.7% of all approaches (Supplementary Figure 12). The tail contained nine of the 15 clustermap selections, against 6.4% of the remaining approaches (Fisher's exact p = 7.8 × 10⁻⁸). We did not reveal ordering of the harshness tiers by this composite score (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.48).

We then compared the biology related metrics of classes L, M and N between the harshness groups and observed clear differences (Table 1; Supplementary Figure 13A–L). Each metric was compared across the three tiers by the Kruskal-Wallis H test and between each pair of tiers by the two-sided Mann-Whitney U test, and the number of approaches behind each metric is given in Supplementary File 1, sheet Harshness_full. Low-harshness methods kept the within-cohort marker correlation close to its maximum (Supplementary Figure 13A) and had the smallest marker-minus-housekeeping contrast (Supplementary Figure 13B). A per-batch linear shift and scale leaves the order of samples within a cohort unchanged, and with it every within-cohort rank correlation, which explains why the low tier saturates class L. High-harshness methods lowered both the same-biology (Supplementary Figure 13C) and the different-biology cross-batch agreement (Supplementary Figure 13D), and they carried the smallest same-minus-different margin (Supplementary Figure 13E) and the smallest margin gain over the unharmonized baseline (Supplementary Figure 13F). Reshaping every sample onto a common distribution removes the rank structure shared by batches of the same biology together with the structure that distinguishes batches of different biology. On LOBO prediction the order reversed: the low tier had the lowest three-class macro F1 on all folds (Supplementary Figure 13G) and on multiclass folds (Supplementary Figure 13H), and the lowest two-class macro F1 on both cuts (Supplementary Figure 13J, K), while the medium and high tiers were close to each other on the multiclass cut (Table 1). Methods that apply a per-batch monotone transform presumably keep, together with the within-cohort ranks, the batch structure that a classifier trained on the other batches never sees, so a held-out batch stays displaced from the training batches. Neither the three-class nor the two-class AUC separated the tiers (Supplementary Figure 13I, L; Table 1). The permutation null lay far below every tier, with medians of 0.237 to 0.261 for the three-class F1 and 0.500 for both AUCs (Supplementary Figure 13G, I, J, L).

Taken together, we showed that prediction quality metrics (class N) are better in medium and high harshness approaches, whereas cross batch agreement by biomarker genes decreases in high harshness. Across all approaches, the class L marker correlation fell as the full-cut three-class LOBO F1 rose (Spearman ρ = −0.234, p = 3.2 × 10⁻²⁹, Supplementary Figure 14A, B). The class M margin, in contrast, rose with the same F1 (ρ = 0.358, p = 2.3 × 10⁻⁶⁸, Supplementary Figure 14C, D). #### Preservation of biomarker expression and of cross-batch rank structure selects a distinct set of approaches

Since none of the 87 scoring metrics in the published benchmark assess expression of biomarker genes, we deeply studied performance of L and M metric classes. These classes were visualized for the clustermap best approach in the benchmarking article but they were not extensively compared between batch removal strategies and harmonization methods. We first plotted harmonized against unharmonized expression of the marker genes BCL6 and IRF4 and of the housekeeping gene EEF1A1 in the four largest cohorts, for four linear and four non-linear methods under the no-removal strategy with KNN imputation and without post-removal (Supplementary Figure 16A–H). The four linear methods, 03_limma, 05_combat, 29_combat_ref and 13_fsmvn, moved every cohort along its own straight line, shifting and rescaling it, so the within-cohort order of the samples was kept exactly (Supplementary Figure 16A–D). Of the non-linear methods, 16_fsqn_r bent the cohort lines while keeping their order (Supplementary Figure 16E), and 17_quantile bent them and added scatter around the curve, which lowers the within-cohort rank correlation slightly (Supplementary Figure 16F). 10_mnn and 12_scanorama returned an almost constant value for each gene in every cohort, so the harmonized expression no longer followed the unharmonized one (Supplementary Figure 16G, H). Class L reads the within-cohort order, which every linear method keeps by construction, and class M reads the cross-batch rank structure, which a collapse onto a constant profile makes identical across batches.

We next examined which approaches class L selects on its own. Five methods returned a median within-cohort marker correlation of exactly 1.000 (01_raw, 36_explobatch, 31_ruv3prps, 27_dwd and 21_harmonizr), and the low-harshness tier had a median of 0.998 (Table 1). Class L therefore favored output that is unchanged or close to unchanged: of its 112 best approaches, 55 are 01_raw and 38 are 36_explobatch. Seven places each went to 13_fsmvn and 02_median_scaling, four to 04_sva and one to 03_limma (Supplementary File 1, sheet Method_census_by_class). Because a marker correlation near 1.000 is reached by leaving the data unchanged, we use a marker correlation above 0.75 as a necessary condition, a gate that an approach must pass before the other classes are applied, and never as a ranking criterion.

We then examined the approaches elected by class M. Its own composite is dominated by same-biology agreement, which rises whenever an algorithm makes all profiles alike, so class M elected only two methods: 10_mnn took 61 of its 112 places and 12_scanorama took 51. The median within-cohort marker correlation of those two methods was −0.001 and −0.008. Both were far below the 0.969 of the other approaches (Mann-Whitney p ≤ 1.7 × 10⁻⁵⁰). Their median same-minus-different-biology margin was 0.000 against 0.043 for the other approaches (p ≤ 2.8 × 10⁻³²). The margin of both methods fell by a median of 0.049 against their own unharmonized baseline (p ≤ 1.4 × 10⁻²¹), and both ranked last by margin (Figure 4C). Both methods collapse each marker gene onto an almost constant value (Supplementary Figure 16G, H), which maximizes same-biology agreement and removes the differences between biological groups at the same time. We therefore read class M through the margin, and through the margin relative to the unharmonized baseline at the same strategy and imputation, which separates an approach that sharpened the biology from one that erased it.

We then applied the two classes together as a joint gate, requiring a marker correlation above 0.75 and a positive margin. Of the 2,234 approaches, 1,686 (75.5%) passed it (Figure 4A, B). Across all approaches the margin rose weakly with the marker correlation (Spearman ρ = 0.177, p = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁷), with the methods separating along both axes (Figure 4A) and the strategies mixed within the gate-passing region (Figure 4B). The methods that passed most often were 06_combat_seq, 03_limma, 07_pycombat, 08_inmoose_combatseq, 01_raw, 02_median_scaling and 05_combat, each in at least 80 of its approaches. By strategy, the RNA-seq-only, FF-only and no-removal strategies passed most often (141, 130 and 130 approaches). The FFPE-only strategy passed least often, in 76 of its 164 approaches. The other strategies passed in 77.8% of their approaches (Fisher's exact p = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁶). The unharmonized output 01_raw passed the joint gate in 80 of its 84 approaches. This was more often than the other approaches (74.7%; Fisher's exact p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁶), so passing the gate does not require having harmonized anything. We then followed the 1,606 gate-passing approaches other than 01_raw from their unharmonized baseline. In these approaches, harmonization raised the median same-biology agreement over the unharmonized baseline by 0.113 in the low tier and 0.110 in the medium tier, and left it unchanged in the high tier (Kruskal–Wallis p = 1.1 × 10⁻²⁹; Figure 4F). The high tier also held most of the approaches that lost more than 0.1 of their agreement: 100 of its 685. The low tier held none of them (Fisher's exact p = 4.4 × 10⁻²⁵).

We then compared batch-removal strategies, imputations and methods on the two class columns (Figure 4C, D). Strategies differed in median marker correlation (Kruskal-Wallis p = 4.1 × 10⁻⁴), from 0.979 under malignant-only down to 0.914 under rare-batches-removed (Figure 4D). They also differed in median margin (p = 3.6 × 10⁻³³), from 0.076 under Affymetrix-only down to 0.009 under FFPE-only (Figure 4D). Only two strategies raised the margin above their own unharmonized baseline in the median, and FFPE-only did so most, by +0.018 (Wilcoxon signed-rank p = 1.2 × 10⁻¹³). RNA-seq-only raised it by +0.008 (p = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴). Eight strategies lowered the margin below their baseline (p ≤ 6.5 × 10⁻⁹ each), and the remaining four changed it by less than 0.001 in the median (Supplementary File 1, sheet Paired_tests). Imputations did not separate the median marker correlation (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.13, Figure 4D). They did separate the median margin (p = 4.4 × 10⁻⁵). The median margin was 0.051 under softimpute, 0.043 under KNN imputation and 0.036 under strict exclusion. Methods separated most: median marker correlation ran from 1.000 for five methods to −0.008 for 12_scanorama (Kruskal-Wallis p < 10⁻³⁰⁰; Supplementary File 1, sheet Paired_tests). Median margin ran from 0.126 for 05_combat to 0.000 for 10_mnn and 12_scanorama (p = 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁹⁴, Figure 4C).

We then required the two directions of class M separately: an approach had to raise same-biology agreement and lower different-biology agreement against its own unharmonized baseline. Only 61 of the 2,150 non-raw approaches (2.8%) did so, and we call them agreement-specific (Figure 4E). They are rare because the two changes were strongly correlated across approaches (Spearman ρ = 0.931, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰), so most approaches moved both agreements in the same direction. They concentrated in the FFPE-only strategy, with 21 approaches. These are 13.3% of its non-raw approaches against 2.0% in the other strategies (Fisher's exact p = 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁰). The microarray-only strategy followed with 11 (p = 2.6 × 10⁻³). The seven approaches of the rare-batches-removed strategy did not exceed its share (p = 0.19). Imputations did not differ in their share (Fisher's exact p ≥ 0.27 for each against the other two). They held 24, 21 and 16 approaches under KNN imputation, softimpute and strict exclusion, respectively. Among methods, 05_combat contributed 14 approaches. These are 17.5% of its non-raw approaches against 2.3% for the other methods (Fisher's exact p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸). Next came 36_explobatch, 06_combat_seq and 08_inmoose_combatseq, with six each (p ≤ 0.028 each). The census by strategy, imputation and method is given in Supplementary File 1, sheet Paired_tests, and the 61 approaches in Supplementary File 2, sheet Agreement_specific_61. The two highest by margin gain are an exact tie, FFPE-only with KNN imputation and no post-removal, under 06_combat_seq and under 08_inmoose_combatseq. Both return a margin of 0.439 and a gain of +0.457 over their own baseline. The gain consists of +0.257 in same-biology agreement and −0.200 in different-biology agreement. The two implementations of ComBat-Seq agree to six decimals at this approach, which serves as a positive control on the class M computation.

We then checked the output scale of the two methods behind these top margins. 06_combat_seq and 08_inmoose_combatseq are also the two methods whose output maxima exceeded their 99th percentiles by seven or more orders of magnitude (Figure 3E), so their best class M values coexist with an output scale far outside the range of the input expression. Classes L and M are built on rank correlations, which do not change when the output is rescaled, so neither class can detect this defect, and a separate check of the output scale is needed next to them.

Marker readouts are known to move with the preparation of the input material, including extracellular-vesicle and plasma-derived inputs [50,51], which is the same axis that the FF-only and FFPE-only strategies separate here. We finally placed Shambhala-2 (20_shambhala) on the two class columns. Its median marker correlation over its 84 approaches was 0.840. This was below the 0.969 of the other approaches (Mann-Whitney p = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁹). Its median margin was 0.005 against 0.043 (p = 7.8 × 10⁻²⁷). None of its approaches entered the agreement-specific group of 61. Its LOBO prediction values on both fold cuts are given in Table 2.

Taken together, the analysis of classes L and M showed that marker correlation saturates for the methods that change expression least, and that cross-batch agreement is highest for the methods that collapse expression onto a constant profile, so neither class can select the best approaches alone. A harmonization benchmarking pipeline should use marker correlation as a gate, read cross-batch agreement through the same-minus-different-biology margin relative to the unharmonized baseline, and check the scale of the harmonized output separately.

#### Cross-batch prediction elects a third set of approaches, led by the ComBat family on both cuts of the fold population

We then evaluated class N, which asks whether a classifier fitted on the batches that are present can label the samples of a batch it has never seen. Over the 2,234 approaches, leave-one-batch-out (LOBO) prediction of the three-class target produced 32,746 held-out folds over 24 RNA batches. Of these folds, 15,436 (47.1%) contained a single biological class. The other 17,310 folds carried two or three classes. Single-class folds reached a median macro F1 of 0.903, against 0.733 for these multiclass folds (Mann–Whitney U, p = 2.3 × 10⁻²²¹; Figure 5C). Among the multiclass folds, two-class folds were harder than three-class folds (median 0.629 against 0.804, Mann–Whitney U, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰; Figure 5C).

We then examined why a held-out batch with a single class scores higher. With one class present, the macro F1 of the fold reduces to the F1 of that class, so the fold tests whether the classifier assigns the diagnosis of the whole held-out cohort and does not test whether it separates diagnoses. The two fold types had close means (0.690 against 0.679) although their medians differed, which places the difference in the shape of the distribution. Single-class batches split into those labeled correctly in the median fold, such as several FF microarray batches at 1.000, and those that failed, such as the FFPE batches GPL887_FFPE_Unknown at 0.000 and GPL13938_FFPE_Unknown at 0.069 (Supplementary File 2, sheet Fold_composition). A mean over all folds therefore mixes the assignment of whole cohorts with the separation of diagnoses measured on the multiclass folds. For this reason we report every class N value on two cuts: the full cut, the mean over all held-out folds, and the multiclass-only cut, the mean over the folds that carry at least two classes.

Before counting the folds, we restricted the fold population to the approaches under analysis. The per-fold table holds 53,737 three-class folds, and 20,991 of them belong to approaches outside the analysis set. Of these, 20,343 come from the 17 non-canonical Shambhala parameter variants. The other 648 come from 34_arsyn and 38_harman, the two methods whose approaches all fail the 5% all-missing criterion. The canonical Shambhala-2 variant, 20_shambhala, is retained and contributes 1,200 of the 32,746 folds.

We then compared the two cuts across approaches. The full-cut and the multiclass-only means correlated with Spearman ρ = 0.810 over the 2,174 approaches carrying both (p < 10⁻³⁰⁰). Over the 2,058 approaches with at least three multiclass folds the correlation was ρ = 0.815 (p < 10⁻³⁰⁰). This agreement is high enough for both cuts to place the same method, 29_combat_ref, first (Figure 5A) and leaves room for the order inside the leading group to change, so both cuts are reported for every approach and group below (Table 2).

We next identified the fold structures under which a high value carries no information on class separation. All 59 approaches without a multiclass fold belong to the Affymetrix-only strategy with post-removal, and 20 of them report a full-cut F1 of at least 0.999; their values rest on single-class folds alone (Figure 5C, D). The strategy that retains the known-bad batches gives the highest full-cut and multiclass-only values among all approaches with a multiclass fold. Under this strategy, strict exclusion with 29_combat_ref and post-removal (A_confirmed_bad / strict / 29_combat_ref / post1) reaches a full-cut F1 of 0.958 and a multiclass-only mean of 0.924 over 11 multiclass folds (Table 2). If a retained bad batch is also diagnosis-pure, held-out prediction measures the confounding between batch and diagnosis, so we report this approach and do not recommend it. Protecting the biological covariate during correction does not remove the problem: when groups are unevenly distributed across batches, the corrected data are not batch-effect free and the estimation errors are deflated [52].

We then defined the eligible set by excluding the two bad-batch-retaining strategies, A_confirmed_bad and B_extended_bad, and the approaches with fewer than five multiclass folds, which left 1,527 approaches. Table 2 gives the named groups as medians and the leading approaches individually, with the F1 of both targets on both fold cuts and the AUC of both targets. The best eligible approach of each of the four leading strategies used 29_combat_ref, except under the microarray-only strategy, where 08_inmoose_combatseq led (Table 2). The leading approach, no removal with softimpute under 29_combat_ref without post-removal (S0_no_removal / softimpute / 29_combat_ref / post0), reached a full-cut F1 of 0.946 and a multiclass-only mean of 0.887 over 11 multiclass folds. Its per-batch detail shows where the two cuts come from (Figure 5B). Of its 24 folds, 13 are single-class. These single-class folds score between 0.986 and 1.000. Its hardest multiclass fold was GPL96_FFPE_Unknown, an FFPE microarray batch of 84 samples and two classes (F1 0.462, AUC 0.543). The next two were the RNA-seq batches RNASeq_FFPE_PolyA (F1 0.698) and RNASeq_FF_PolyA (F1 0.718; Supplementary File 2, sheet Per_batch_folds). Two of the three hardest folds are therefore FFPE batches, presumably because degraded FFPE material shifts expression profiles away from those of the training batches.

We then examined the elected set of class N, its 112 highest-scoring approaches. Class N resulted in the ComBat family: four implementations of that algorithm, 05_combat, 29_combat_ref, 06_combat_seq and 08_inmoose_combatseq, took 84 of the 112 places, and eight further methods shared the rest (Supplementary File 1, sheet Method_census_by_class). Across the analysis set, the ComBat family holds 277 approaches. They reached a median full-cut F1 of 0.873 against 0.669 for the other methods (Mann–Whitney U, p = 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁰⁵). The difference held on the multiclass-only cut (0.771 against 0.665, Mann–Whitney U, p = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴⁴). Methods differed on the full cut (Kruskal–Wallis H = 1,425, p = 4.3 × 10⁻²⁸¹; Figure 5A). Their medians ran from 0.898 for 29_combat_ref to 0.453 for 02_median_scaling. The same two methods were first and last on the multiclass-only cut (0.827 against 0.571, Kruskal–Wallis p = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁶⁹). The five last methods on the full cut, 02_median_scaling, 03_limma, 07_pycombat, 21_harmonizr and 28_npn, include four of the six methods that drive batch PCReg to saturation (Figure 5A), consistent with the removal of diagnosis-associated variance together with the batch-associated one. 07_pycombat, a Python implementation of ComBat, is one of these four and received no place in the class N elected set (Supplementary File 1, sheet Method_census_by_class).

Two fold structures described above carry into the class N elected set. Twenty of its approaches use a bad-batch-retaining strategy, 15 of them A_confirmed_bad, which is more than that strategy's share of the analysis set (Fisher exact test, p = 0.024). Five further elected approaches have fewer than five multiclass folds, and three of these have none. In total, 25 of the 112 elected places rest on these fold structures.

The elected set spread over 13 of the 14 strategies. It included no FFPE-only approach, although that strategy holds 164 approaches of the analysis set (Fisher exact test, p = 2.7 × 10⁻⁴). Its imputation mix departed weakly from that of the analysis set (χ² goodness-of-fit test, p = 0.042). It held 50 approaches with strict exclusion, 33 with softimpute and 29 with KNN imputation. Its 63 approaches with post-removal did not differ from the half expected from the analysis set (two-sided binomial test, p = 0.22).

We then compared the pipeline factors on both cuts across all approaches (Supplementary File 1, sheet Paired_tests). Strategies separated the full-cut F1 (Kruskal–Wallis p = 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁷), with group medians from 0.763 for Affymetrix-extended to 0.561 for FFPE-only. Imputations separated it less (Kruskal–Wallis p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁴), with group medians spanning 0.012. The effect sizes order the three factors: ε² was 0.634 across methods, 0.044 across strategies and 0.007 across imputations. On the multiclass-only cut, strategy medians ran from 0.724 for microarray-only to 0.462 for Affymetrix-only (Kruskal–Wallis p = 3.7 × 10⁻⁸⁰). The Affymetrix-only median rests on the 95 of its 154 approaches that carry a multiclass fold at all. Imputation medians spanned 0.050 on that cut, from strict exclusion down to KNN imputation (Kruskal–Wallis p = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁴). Post-removal raised the full-cut median from 0.692 to 0.719 (Mann–Whitney U, p = 2.9 × 10⁻³). It did not change the multiclass-only median (0.669 against 0.681, Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.13), which places its gain in the single-class folds. Shambhala-2 (20_shambhala) sat above the other approaches on the full cut (Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.012). Its median over 83 approaches was 0.745, against 0.707 for the other approaches. It did not differ from them on the multiclass-only cut (0.700 against 0.674, Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.10; Table 2).

We controlled every class N value with 100 permutations of the biological labels. Under permutation, the full-cut three-class macro F1 fell to tier medians between 0.237 and 0.261, against an observed median of 0.709 over all approaches (Supplementary Figure 13G). The permuted two-class F1 fell to tier medians between 0.393 and 0.441 (Supplementary Figure 13J). The permuted AUC stayed at 0.500 for both targets in every tier, the value expected from a classifier without information (Supplementary Figure 13I, L). The observed full-cut three-class F1 exceeded its permutation distribution at p ≤ 0.05 in 2,197 of the 2,233 approaches carrying the column. The median permutation p was 0.0099 in each tier, the smallest value that 100 permutations can give. The differences between methods in class N are therefore differences between classifiers that almost all predict above chance.

We then combined six prediction and preservation columns into the generalizability index, the mean of six rank percentiles: the full-cut and multiclass-only three-class macro F1, the full-cut two-class macro F1, the class L marker correlation, the class M margin gain over the unharmonized baseline and the worst multiclass fold. Over the whole analysis set, the 15 highest index values, from 0.949 to 0.986, all belong to Affymetrix-only approaches with post-removal and no multiclass fold. A missing column leaves the mean over the columns that remain, and the prediction columns of these approaches rest on single-class folds alone (Figure 5D). Restricted to the 1,527 eligible approaches, the index is led by no removal with strict exclusion under 29_combat_ref with post-removal (S0_no_removal / strict / 29_combat_ref / post1) at 0.886 (Table 3). All 15 leading eligible approaches are ComBat implementations, nine under 29_combat_ref and six under 05_combat, and nine of the 15 use the FF-only strategy (Table 3). The index followed the full-cut three-class F1 closely (Spearman ρ = 0.829, p < 10⁻³⁰⁰, n = 2,233; Figure 5D). The 15 clustermap-selected approaches ranked between 433rd and 2,022nd of 2,234 on the index. Their median index did not differ from that of the other approaches (0.466 against 0.476, Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.95).

#### The sets elected by the individual metric classes show limited intersection

We then ran the election once per class over the same 2,234 approaches, which gave eight sets of 112 approaches, and compared them with each other and with the 15 approaches of the published clustermap selection (Table 4; Supplementary File 2, sheet Cross_election_pairs). Every overlap below was tested against this expectation with a one-sided hypergeometric test in the direction observed. Taken together, the four batch-facing classes and the composite agreed above chance in nine of their ten pairs, whereas the three biology-facing classes shared fewer approaches than chance with each other and elected sets built on different methods (Table 4). A benchmark that selects harmonization approaches should therefore declare its metric classes, its gates and its held-out test before ranking, and report the elected set of every class with the intersections and their chance expectation. Class L then serves as a gate, because a per-batch monotone transform keeps within-cohort marker ranks: the low-harshness tier holds a median marker correlation of 0.998 while predicting worst of the three tiers on the full cut (Table 1). Class M is read as the margin against the unharmonized baseline at the same strategy and imputation, because agreement alone rewards algorithms that make all profiles alike. Class N is reported on both fold cuts with the multiclass fold count, because 47.1% of the held-out batches carry one class, and with the bad-batch-retaining strategies excluded, because they reach the highest values of all (Table 2). ### Conclusions

We elected the top 5% of 2,234 harmonization approaches once for each of eight metric classes, changing only the class that ranked them, and compared the elected sets with the 15 approaches selected by the published clustermap. The largest overlap between two elected sets of 112 approaches was 71 shared approaches (Jaccard 0.464), between the local class and the composite that contains it (Table 4). The three biology-facing classes shared one approach in total, the unharmonized I_rare_batches_removed / softimpute / 01_raw / post1 elected by classes L and N, and each of their three pairwise overlaps was below the 5.6 approaches expected by chance. The clustermap selection shared no approach with classes L and N, which a set of 15 does by chance with probability 0.46. On the generalizability index its approaches ranked between 433rd and 2,022nd of 2,234.

Each class elected the approaches that maximize the quantity it measures. Marker preservation (class L) elected unharmonized and near-unharmonized output, because a per-batch monotone transform leaves within-cohort ranks unchanged. Cross-batch rank agreement (class M) elected 10_mnn and 12_scanorama, which make profiles alike and return median marker correlations of −0.001 and −0.008. The global class elected 03_limma and 13_fsmvn, two of the six methods that drive batch PCReg to saturation, and 13_fsmvn predicted held-out batches worse than the other methods (median full-cut F1 0.537 against 0.715, Mann–Whitney U, p = 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁸). Cross-batch prediction (class N) elected four ComBat implementations, two of which, 06_combat_seq and 08_inmoose_combatseq, return mean maximum expression values of 2.76 × 10⁸ and 1.46 × 10¹¹ (Figure 3E).

Three properties of the metrics themselves caused most of this behavior, and each can be handled at the reporting stage. A within-cohort correlation metric saturates, so it works as a gate at a stated threshold and does not order the approaches that pass it: the low-harshness tier holds a median marker correlation of 0.998 while returning the lowest full-cut prediction F1 of the three tiers, 0.631 (Table 1). An agreement metric rises whenever an algorithm makes profiles alike, so it has to be read as the margin between same-biology and different-biology agreement, taken against the unharmonized baseline at the same strategy and imputation. Requiring both directions of that margin leaves 61 of the 2,150 non-raw approaches (2.8%). A prediction metric depends on the composition of the held-out batch, so it has to be cut by batch and reported on both fold cuts: 15,436 of the 32,746 three-class folds carry one class. These single-class folds reach a median macro F1 of 0.903 against 0.733 for the rest (Mann–Whitney U, p = 2.3 × 10⁻²²¹). The highest prediction values among approaches with a multiclass fold come from a strategy that keeps the known-bad batches (full-cut F1 0.958, multiclass-only mean 0.924; Table 2).

We set these steps out as a recipe (Figure 7). First, declare the metric classes before computing them. Second, compute every class over the full approach set. Third, use the within-cohort marker correlation as a gate at a stated threshold. Fourth, rank on the class M margin taken against the unharmonized baseline at the same strategy and imputation. Fifth, validate by leave-one-batch-out prediction whose projection is fitted on the training batches only, reported on both fold cuts with the multiclass fold count beside each value. Sixth, report the elected set of every class together with their intersections, so that a reader can see which recommendation depends on which criterion. Applied to this dataset, 1,187 approaches pass the gate with a positive margin and carry at least five multiclass folds outside the bad-batch-retaining strategies. The leading one by full-cut F1 is no removal with softimpute under 29_combat_ref without post-removal (S0_no_removal / softimpute / 29_combat_ref / post0), with a full-cut F1 of 0.946 and a marker correlation of 0.880 (Table 2). Its multiclass-only mean was 0.887 over 11 multiclass folds.

The limits of this work follow from its design. All 2,234 approaches come from one dataset of germinal-center B-cell lymphomas, so the numbers are specific to a setting in which the biological contrast is small and the technical contrast is large. The election threshold of 5% is a choice, and a different threshold changes the set sizes. The ordering of the overlaps did not depend on it, and all 36 pairs at each threshold are listed in Supplementary File 2, sheet Threshold_sensitivity. The local and composite sets overlapped most at thresholds of 2.5%, 5% and 10%. At the 2.5% threshold, the pairwise Jaccard values correlated with those at 5% (Spearman ρ = 0.801). At the 10% threshold, they correlated with ρ = 0.881. Classes L and M rest on one 633-gene panel, and a different panel would change their elected sets. The prediction class uses three diagnostic classes and 24 batches, and nearly half of its held-out folds carry one class, so its folds are coarse. Because every class was elected from the same 2,234 approaches by the same procedure, the differences between the elected sets arise from the metric classes themselves.
